Below ground, under a lid

Bees in a Meter, Valve or Irrigation Box in Miami-Dade

A plastic or concrete box sunk in the lawn is dark, dry on top and about the size a small colony wants. Below: who owns which box, why the lid is the risky part, and what a removal involves.

Quick answer

Leave the lid where it is. A colony inside a meter, valve or pool equipment box usually hangs its comb from the underside of that lid, so lifting it tears the nest open in your hands. Figure out who owns the box first: the water meter near the street is the utility's, while irrigation valves, sub-meters and pool pits are usually yours. Then call or text us a photo taken from a few steps back. Every bee leaves by gentle vacuum, still living, bound for a beekeeper's apiary.

If anyone is being stung

If bees are boiling out of a box and someone is being stung, walk away fast in a straight line and get indoors or into a car. Do not swat or jump into a pool. Once you are safe, get the stingers out by dragging a card edge across the skin. Trouble breathing, swelling around the eyes or mouth, or a sting count in the dozens is a 911 call. After that, call us about the colony.

What you might be noticing at ground level

  • Bees streaming through the finger hole of a lid in the grass, or squeezing in at a cracked corner.
  • Someone who reads or services the meter mentioned bees at your box, or backed away from it.
  • Bees rising around your ankles when you mow or edge along the sidewalk, always near the same spot.
  • A low hum when you stand over the sprinkler valve box or the pool pump pit, even with the system off.
  • Dark, sticky staining or wax flakes around the rim of a lid, and ants gathering there.

What is going on behind it

A scout bee judges a cavity by darkness, dryness and size, and a buried box passes on all three. The lid keeps rain off, the walls block wind, and the opening is small enough to guard. Workers build comb downward from the lid, then out along pipes, valve bodies or wiring. Within weeks the box can hold brood, stored honey and thousands of bees. Because the colony sits at foot level beside a walkway or lawn, people walk straight into its flight path. Meter boxes appear by name, next to old tires and cement blocks, in the extension service's list of spots where African honey bees have nested, and those bees defend even a small nest hard. You cannot tell which kind you have by looking, so treat every boxed colony as one that may sting.

Whose box is it, and why that comes first

Not every lid in your yard is yours. The Miami-Dade Water and Sewer Department notes that the water meter usually sits in the front yard near the street or sidewalk, and its rules treat that service meter as the department's. Whoever supplies your water, if bees are in the meter box, report it to that utility before anyone opens it. Florida law makes it a crime to willfully alter, tamper with or damage a utility's meter, pipes or wires, and that applies to the people removing bees too. Our crew will not unbolt, cut or move a utility's meter, valve or cable. Irrigation valve boxes, a separate sub-meter for sprinklers or pool filling, and pool equipment pits are normally on the customer's side, so those can be opened once you say so. Electrical and phone pull boxes vary, and the lettering cast into the lid often names the service or the company behind it. If you rent, your landlord may need to approve the work; see who handles bees in a rental.

Why the lid is the hard part

In a wall or a roof the comb is hidden behind something you can cut and patch. In a buried box the lid is both the door and the ceiling, and the comb is attached to it. Lift it fast and slabs of brood and honey break off, drop into the box or onto the grass, and the whole colony comes up at once. That is the moment people get stung. A slow, controlled lift with the bees calmed first keeps the comb in one piece so it can be cut free and carried off with the bees. Mud, sand or a sprinkler leak can also make the box wet underneath, so the bottom layer of comb may already be soft and moldy. Our removal request form has room for a note about the lid material and any leaks you've noticed.

Keeping kids, pets and mowers away until then

A ground-level colony is easy to bump without knowing it. Mark off the area with a chair or a cone a few steps back from the box, and pause the mowing and edging service until the bees are out. Run the sprinkler zone fed by that valve as little as you can, since vibration and water both stir the colony. Keep dogs indoors or on the far side of the yard, because they tend to sniff at the hole. If the box sits by a front walk, use another door for now. These steps cost nothing and they keep the problem confined to the lid. When you are not sure where the bees are actually living, a thermal camera can show whether the colony is in the box itself or in a pipe chase or wall next to it.

Getting the colony up and out of the ground

  1. Confirm ownership

    We check the lid markings and where the box sits, and if it is the utility's, the utility is contacted before anything is touched.

  2. Calm, then lift

    A light puff of smoke settles the bees at the entrance, and the lid is raised slowly with the comb still attached so it does not tear.

  3. Vacuum the bees

    A low-suction bee vacuum draws the workers into a ventilated box, so the colony leaves the lid and pipes alive and together.

  4. Cut out every comb

    Brood and honeycomb are cut from the lid, walls, pipes and valve bodies, then tied into frames so the brood travels with its colony.

  5. Scrub the box, seat the lid

    Wax and honey are scraped and washed out of the box, the gaps are dealt with, and the lid goes back on firmly.

What we put back afterward

A valve or meter box that still smells of honey will pull scouts back, so its inside gets scraped and rinsed down to the plastic or concrete, and spilled comb is lifted out of the soil underneath. Cracked lids and broken corners on your own boxes can be replaced, and gaps where pipes or conduit enter can be filled or screened. Should a box we closed up attract a new colony, let us know: the warranty on our workmanship covers a return trip. The guide on returning colonies explains how lingering scent pulls new swarms in, and for the residue itself see comb and honey cleanup.

What moves the price on this job

Price here follows the dig, not the ladder. One box with a clean lid is simple ground-level work, generally about $300 to $400. Comb that has crept down a conduit, under a pool deck or into a wall, a box or lid that cracks and has to be swapped, or waiting on a utility to clear its equipment all push the total up, sometimes into the thousands once repairs join in. Questions people ask us

Would bleach or bug spray poured through the finger hole kill them off?

Please don't. Spraying or flooding a buried box kills the bees, leaves rotting comb and honey that draws ants and new swarms, and may damage the meter or valve. On a utility's box it can also count as interfering with its equipment. Live removal clears the bees and the comb together.

What if the colony is inside the water meter box by the street?

Report it to your water utility first, which for many homes is the Miami-Dade Water and Sewer Department, since the service meter is theirs. Ask how they want it handled and pass along anything they tell you about access. Until then, keep people back and don't lift the lid yourself.

Is my irrigation sub-meter box my responsibility?

The water department's guidelines put access, cleanout and broken box or cover replacement on the customer for a sub-meter, so in practice that box is yours to keep clear. Bees in it are something you can schedule directly. See also live honey bee removal.

Will the bees damage my pool pump or valves?

The bees don't chew through equipment, but comb, honey and wax can gum up valve handles, timers and electrical fittings, and a pump left running shakes the colony up. Shut that equipment off if you can and leave the pit closed. For screen cages and pump areas, see pool enclosures and equipment.

Our buyer's inspection report mentions bees under a sprinkler valve lid. What should we do?

Get a quote, and ask for the itemized invoice and photos to keep for the buyer's file. Work at a single lid like this tends to stay modest in scope. For closing deadlines and what buyers usually ask for, read bees found during a sale.
