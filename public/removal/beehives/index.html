Quick answer

A hive is a colony that has already built comb in a fixed spot, often a wall, roof, soffit or hollow tree. Unlike a passing swarm, it is unlikely to leave on its own. Humane beehive removal in Miami means finding the comb, opening the surface over it, moving the bees and their brood comb to a beekeeper, taking out the honey and wax, then sealing and repairing the opening. The bees live. Your structure gets put back together.

If anyone is being stung

Bees streaming out and stinging? First, put walls between the bees and everybody nearby, pets included; a car with the windows up works too. Leave the entrance alone: spraying, blocking or soaking it tends to make a colony angrier and can push bees inside. Once everyone is under a roof, call. Signs you have a hive, not a swarm

  • Bees have been flying in and out of the same crack, vent or gap for more than a few days, in a steady line.
  • A low, steady hum comes through a wall or ceiling and does not fade over the week.
  • A dark, greasy stain is spreading on paint or stucco near where the bees go in.
  • Bees turn up indoors, crawling on the ceiling or gathering at windows and light fixtures.
  • Honey or melted wax has dripped from a soffit, a ceiling joint or a crack in the stucco.
  • A cluster you saw last week on a branch is now gone, but bees keep working a hole in the eave nearby.

What is going on behind it

Picture a swarm as a colony between addresses: the old queen plus a big share of her workforce, hanging on a branch with no wax at all, waiting for scouts to report back. It is usually gone soon. A hive is what happens after the scouts choose. The workers start drawing wax comb inside the cavity, the queen begins laying eggs, and the space fills with brood, pollen and stored honey. From that point the colony is defending a nest it has invested in, and it has no reason to leave. That is why an established colony needs a cut-out, where the comb itself is removed, rather than a pickup. The longer it stays, the larger the comb gets and the more of the structure has to be opened to reach it.

What leftover wax does to a house

Moving the bees only solves half the problem. Comb left behind in a wall or roof keeps working against your house. With no bees fanning and cooling it, wax softens and honey can seep into drywall, wood and plaster, leaving a stain that is hard to paint over. Abandoned brood dies and decays, and the stored honey ferments, so the odor builds. The comb itself becomes food for wax moth and small hive beetle larvae, and roaches and other insects come for the rotting brood. Old comb also attracts bees from neighboring colonies, and a cavity that already smells of a nest is an easy pick for a future swarm. University of Florida extension guidance for removals is plain about it: take out all the comb, then seal the entry points. Our comb and honey cleanup covers the part that is easy to skip and expensive to regret.

Cut-out or trap-out

Some removers try to coax a colony out of a wall with a one-way cone over the entrance, leaving the comb where it is. That can work for a few colonies in a few spots, but it leaves the wax and honey inside, which brings back every problem above. Plugging the hole from outside is worse: the bees look for another way out, and in a house that is often an outlet, a vent or a gap around a light fixture, into your living room. For a colony that has built comb, a cut-out is the clean answer. The opening is sized to the nest, and a thermal scan maps the heat of the brood area first, so the first cut lands in the right place. If you are not sure yet whether you have a swarm or a hive, the swarm pickup page explains the difference from the other side.

Where established colonies end up

A scouting party grades a cavity on volume, shelter from rain and a narrow way in. Plenty of Miami-Dade construction scores well on all three. Hollow block walls and frame walls (bees in walls), the space under roof tile or inside a flat roof (roof hives), boxed soffits and eaves (soffit and eave colonies), and the hollow trunks of old trees (palm and tree colonies) all qualify. So do storage sheds and the back corners of garages. Each one comes apart differently, which is why the removal plan starts with finding exactly where the comb is, not with a pry bar.

How a beehive cut-out is done

  1. Find the edges of the comb

    We scan the wall, ceiling or roof with a thermal camera and listen at the surface, so the opening matches the size and shape of the nest instead of guessing.

  2. Open the surface

    Drywall, soffit panels, roof tile or siding comes off over the comb. Where the surface can be saved and refit, we take it off in pieces that can go back.

  3. Move the bees alive

    Low suction draws the adult bees off the comb into an airy carrier. Finding the queen matters most, since the rest will settle wherever she ends up.

  4. Band the brood comb

    Sheets of comb with eggs and developing bees are trimmed to size and tied into hive frames, giving the beekeeper a colony that can rebuild quickly.

  5. Clear every scrap

    Honey comb, empty wax and debris are cut back to the framing and bagged. The cavity is scraped and cleaned so nothing is left to ferment or attract the next swarm.

What we put back afterward

An empty cavity still has a door the next colony can use, so that door gets shut. Cracks and gaps the bees traveled through are packed with filler or caulk, and vents keep their airflow behind fine mesh. Rebuilding stays in-house too. Whichever drywall panel, soffit run or tile course came apart is restored by our own licensed contractors and roofers, and our painters finish it to blend in. That workmanship carries a warranty with no fixed term attached; should a colony take up residence where we closed things off, a crew returns. Want a record for insurance or a landlord? What moves the price on this job

Reach decides a lot. A nest you can get to standing on the ground, say low in a block wall or inside a shed, typically comes in near $300 to $400. Comb that runs up a second-story wall, sits beneath roof tile or spreads across a ceiling means more cutting, more labor and more rebuilding, so once repairs are counted the bill may reach the thousands. Questions people ask us

If I cannot see any comb, how do I know it is a hive?

Time and traffic tell you. A swarm hangs in the open, often on a branch or a fence, and is usually gone in a day or a few. Bees that keep flying into the same gap in a wall or roof for a week or more have almost certainly built comb behind it. A thermal scan confirms where.

Can you remove the hive without opening anything?

No, at least not with the wax going too. The bees can sometimes be drawn out through the entrance, but the wax, honey and dead brood stay inside, and those are what cause stains, smells, pests and repeat swarms. Some opening is needed, and we keep it to the size of the nest.

Collecting a swarm and cutting out a hive are priced differently. What drives the gap?

A swarm is a cluster on the outside of something, collected into a box with nothing to cut. A hive means opening part of the building, removing comb by hand, cleaning the cavity and rebuilding. More time, more trades and more materials go into it, which is why repairs push some jobs into the thousands.

Once the hive is gone, could another colony claim that cavity?

It is possible when wax residue or honey odor lingers, or when the old doorway stays open, and that is the reason for scraping the space bare and closing each gap. Should a new colony settle behind work our crew closed up, that warranty sends someone out again. The field guide covers why bees come back to one spot and what scouting bees are hunting for.

Our condo board needs paperwork. Can you provide it?

Yes. We carry a license and insurance, and the COI your manager asks for can be emailed ahead of the work date, before anyone sets up a ladder. Pictures and an itemized bill follow if the board wants them for its records. Building managers juggling board votes and gate access should also read our page for associations and commercial sites.

A home inspector found a hive. What now?

Get it out and the opening rebuilt before closing, so the buyer does not take on comb inside a wall. A free quote, photos and an itemized invoice give buyer and seller the same paper trail.
