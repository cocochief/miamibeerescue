The method behind every job

Moving Miami Bees Out Alive, Nest and Young Included

A honey bee colony is a nest of wax, honey, eggs and young bees, not just the cloud at the entrance. Live removal means taking all of it out of your building in one piece and handing it to a beekeeper.

Quick answer

Live removal takes the whole nest out of your wall, roof or tree without spraying anything. The comb is located first, often with a thermal camera, and then the cavity is opened just wide enough to reach it. Each sheet of brood comb is cut free and fastened into a frame in a hive box, the adult bees are drawn off with a gentle vacuum, and the queen travels with them. The box goes to a beekeeper's yard, and the empty cavity is cleaned and sealed so new scouts find nothing to move into.

If anyone is being stung

A living colony guards its brood, so a knocked or disturbed nest can send bees after people. Lead whoever is being chased indoors, or into a vehicle with the windows up, and scrape each stinger out with a fingernail. Swelling lips or tongue, or wheezing, means dialing 911 first. Then ring us at whatever hour it is: someone always picks up, and an active stinging call is dealt with before any scheduled removal.

Signs you are dealing with a living colony

  • A steady line of bees flying in and out of one crack, vent or tile gap, at the same spot day after day.
  • Bees standing at the entrance on a hot afternoon with their wings blurred, fanning to cool the nest behind them.
  • A low hum through the drywall or ceiling when you put your ear close, loudest in the warm middle of the day.
  • A dark stain or a sticky trickle below the entry point, a sign of honey-filled comb inside.
  • Bees landing with yellow or orange pollen packed on their back legs, which means there are young inside to feed.

What is going on behind it

Those signs point to a settled colony, not a resting swarm. Once a swarm picks a cavity, the workers start building wax comb right away, and the queen begins laying in the first cells they finish. A worker takes about three weeks to go from egg to adult, so the brood is the colony's future: without it, the bees you see would simply age out. The outer comb fills with honey and pollen, and the whole nest is held near body heat by the bees themselves. That is why the method matters. Take the bees and leave the comb, and you have a cavity full of food with no one guarding it. Take the comb and bees together, and the colony survives somewhere else while your building is left clean.

Why spraying a hive makes the job bigger

Poison kills the bees in the cavity, but it does nothing about the nest they built. A wall colony can hold many pounds of comb and honey, and with no workers fanning it, that wax softens in a Miami summer. Honey can then seep through drywall, stain a ceiling or drip into a light fixture. Dead brood rots and smells. Scavengers arrive fast: wax moth larvae tunnel through the abandoned comb, small hive beetles breed in it, and ants follow the sugar.

The leftover comb also keeps its scent, and that scent is exactly what scouting bees search for when a new swarm needs a home. It is a large part of the reason colonies keep reappearing in a cavity that was sprayed years earlier. Lift the comb out with the living bees on it and none of those problems has anything to feed on. There is no pesticide in your walls, around your kids or pets, or in the honey that a neighbor's bees might rob.

Why the entry hole is rarely where we open

The hole the bees use is not always on top of the nest. Bees will walk a surprising distance inside a wall, down a roof rafter or along a soffit before they reach their comb, so cutting at the entrance can mean a second, larger opening later.

A colony keeps its brood area warm, a little below the temperature of a human body, and that heat shows up on a thermal camera as a bright patch through stucco, drywall or tile. Our guide on what thermal imaging shows goes through the details. Listening and light tapping fill in the edges. The goal is a single, tidy cut placed over the comb, sized so the repair stays small. The same mapping step comes first whether the colony is in a block or frame wall, under roof tile or behind a soffit panel.

Moving bees and brood together

Once the cavity is open, the comb comes out one sheet at a time. Sheets that hold eggs, larvae and capped brood are trimmed to fit and held in empty wooden frames with bands or string, kept upright the way the bees built them. Within a few days the workers attach the comb to the frame on their own.

Comb that is mostly honey is set aside, since it is heavy and drips, and it is handled through comb and honey cleanup. The adult bees are collected with a low-suction vacuum that drops them into the same box as their brood. The queen is either spotted and caged or gathered with the cluster. Foragers that were out during the work return to the old spot, so a final pass at dusk picks up the stragglers.

Florida's agriculture department notes that African and European honey bees cannot be told apart by eye, only by genetic testing, so every wild colony is handled with the same care.

How a live colony comes out

  1. Map the nest

    A thermal scan and careful listening find the warm mass of comb, so the opening goes over the nest and not beside it.

  2. Open the cavity

    Stucco, drywall, a soffit panel or a run of tiles comes off in one section, just big enough to reach the edges of the comb. Cool smoke keeps the bees calm.

  3. Frame the brood

    Each sheet of brood comb is cut out, fitted into a frame and set in a ventilated hive box, upright, so the young bees keep developing.

  4. Collect the adults

    A gentle vacuum gathers the bees and the queen into the box with their brood, and an evening pass collects foragers coming home.

  5. Hand off to a beekeeper

    The closed box rides to a beekeeper's yard, where the colony is moved into permanent woodenware and allowed to settle.

What we put back afterward

When the last comb is out, the cavity is scraped down to bare surfaces, because wax residue and old honey are what draw the next swarm. Closing up stays in-house: our own licensed roofers, painters and contractors rebuild the stucco, drywall, tile or trim to match its surroundings, and the original entry gap gets sealed so it cannot be reused. That seal carries our workmanship warranty, and a colony that settles in behind it earns you a return visit. Ask, and you get pictures taken before the cavity was closed, along with the finished repair.

What moves the price on this job

Live removal is more work than a spray, since the nest has to be found, opened and lifted out piece by piece. A nest you can reach from the ground, such as a hedge, a planter or a low wall, comes in around $300 to $400. A big colony under roof tile or deep in a second-floor wall, with rebuilding and paint to follow, reaches the thousands. Questions people ask us

Can't you just spray the hole and seal it shut?

Sealing a live colony in usually backfires. Bees shut inside a wall crawl toward any light they can find, and inside your home that can be an outlet cover or a ceiling fixture. Spraying first leaves the comb behind to melt, rot and attract pests. Taking the colony out alive, then sealing an empty, cleaned cavity, is the only order that actually ends the problem.

Does the queen really survive the move?

She lives on the brood comb, so she goes into the box with it or gets caged when she is spotted. If she is lost in the process, the colony is not finished: workers can raise a new queen from a worker larva less than about three days old. That is one more reason the brood always travels with the bees instead of being thrown out.

After the colony is boxed up, what becomes of it?

From your property the colony is driven to a beekeeper's apiary. The keeper watches its temperament over the following weeks and can replace the queen of a colony that turns out to be defensive. Our colony relocation page covers that hand-off in more detail.

Is it legal in Florida to kill a honey bee colony?

Florida treats the two jobs as separate trades. State rules expect beekeepers who do live removal for hire to hold a beekeeper registration with the Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services, while eradication is reserved for pest control companies licensed under Chapter 482. This service only does live removal: the colony is never killed.

How long does taking out a live colony take?

It depends on the size of the nest and where it sits. A small colony in a shrub or an open box can be boxed in one visit. A large wall or roof colony can fill most of a day, and a short return at dusk may be needed to collect foragers that were away during the work.

Could scouts pick my house again next season?

They can, if wax or honey residue stays behind or the gap stays open, since scouts home in on that smell. A scraped cavity and a sealed entry take away both lures. Learn how scout bees inspect a house so you can recognize a few curious bees early, before a full swarm arrives.
