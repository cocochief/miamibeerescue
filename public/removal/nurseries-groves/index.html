Feral Bee Colonies on Miami-Dade Farms, Groves and Nurseries

A wild colony in a valve box or a packing shed wall is a crew safety problem first. This is how it leaves your land alive, and why the boxes your beekeeper set out stay put.

Quick answer

A feral honey bee colony that has settled in a pump house, an irrigation valve box, a shade house post, a parked tractor or the trunk of an old grove tree can come out alive and go home with a beekeeper. Your workers are kept out of a marked zone, nearby engines go quiet, every piece of comb is cut out and the cavity is closed. Hives a beekeeper placed for pollination are another matter entirely: they have an owner, and they are not ours to move.

If anyone is being stung

If someone on your crew is being stung right now, get them into a closed truck cab or building, away from the nest. A canal or ditch is no escape, because the bees hover over the water and wait. Call 911 for many stings, a swollen face or lips, or any trouble breathing. Do not walk back to switch off equipment beside the nest. Then call us: a person comes before a pump.

What your crew keeps telling you

  • Bees flying a tight line into a crack in the pump house wall, or slipping under the lid of an irrigation valve box.
  • Workers getting stung the moment they crank a mower, a pump engine or a sprayer, even though nobody touched the nest.
  • Comb hanging under a potting bench, inside a stack of empty nursery containers, or along a frame member of a shade house.
  • Bees spilling out of a knot hole or a split low on the trunk of an older mango or avocado tree in the grove.
  • A colony packed into the cab, toolbox or engine housing of a tractor or trailer that sat unused for a few weeks.
  • A dense cluster on a pallet or a wall in the packing shed, and staff refusing to work that end of the building.

What is going on behind it

Scout bees hunt for a dry, dark, sheltered space of about the right size, and a farm offers them dozens: valve boxes, hollow pipe, block pump houses, stacked pots, idle machinery, rotted heartwood in a grove tree. Once a swarm moves in, the workers build comb fast and start storing honey. What makes this dangerous on a farm is noise and vibration. A colony reads a pump starting or a mower passing a few feet away as an attack and sends defenders out. Florida's agriculture department says genetic testing is required to tell Africanized bees from European ones, since the eye cannot, so a colony that reacts hard should be treated as a real hazard. Any colony with no box and no beekeeper attached is feral, and that is the kind we take.

Feral colonies versus the beekeeper's boxes

Some growers rent hives during bloom, and those boxes sit on pallets at the edge of a block for a reason. Under Florida law every beekeeper has to register with FDACS, the state agriculture agency, and a managed colony belongs to whoever placed it. If bees are bothering your crew near those boxes, the call goes to that beekeeper, not to a removal crew.

A feral colony is different. It chose your pump house or your tree on its own, nobody tends it, and it will keep growing until someone takes it out. A loose swarm hanging from a fence post or a shade cloth seam is the in-between case: it has left home and has not settled yet, and a swarm pickup can collect it before scouts pick a cavity inside one of your structures. If you are not sure which you are looking at, text us a photo and say so.

Keeping workers safe while the colony is still there

Until the colony is out, treat the spot like a hazard on the property map. Flag it, tell every crew lead, and move work to another block if the nest sits beside a path people use. Keep mowers, weed eaters, pumps and sprayers off within a generous distance, because the noise and vibration of a running engine close to the nest can set a colony off. Do not open the valve box to look, do not spray it with pesticide or water, and do not try to burn or smoke it out of a tree.

Anyone with a known sting allergy should stay well away. If the colony sits next to a pump you cannot shut down for long, mention that when you call, since phones are answered 24/7 and the emergency line puts people being stung ahead of everything else.

Why nurseries and groves give bees so many options

South Dade is ornamental plant country. The county extension office counts more than 2,400 nurseries registered with the state in 2024, says most ornamental plants here grow in shade houses or open fields, and notes that ornamental crops use about 35 percent of the county's farmland while bringing in more than 80 percent of its farm sales. Add groves of avocado, mango, lychee and longan, plus irrigation running through the dry months, and bees find flowers, water and shelter on the same parcel.

That is also why one removal does not settle things for good if openings stay open. Old comb scent draws new scouts, a point the guide on returning bees covers, and storm damage can expose or create new cavities, as the after-a-storm guide explains.

How a farm removal is done

  1. Walk the site first

    Your manager shows us where crews, pumps and vehicles will be that day. We set a perimeter and agree on which engines stay off until the colony is boxed.

  2. Map the nest by its heat

    The brood nest runs warm, so thermal imaging shows its outline through a pump house wall, hollow post or grove trunk. That warm patch tells us where to open up, and how little.

  3. Cut comb, collect bees

    Comb is lifted out piece by piece while a low-suction vacuum pulls the bees into a vented carrier. If the queen turns up, she rides with them.

  4. Clear every trace of honey

    All wax and honey leave the cavity so robbing bees, ants and beetles are not drawn in, and the scent that pulls the next swarm is gone.

  5. A new home with a beekeeper

    The boxed colony leaves with us and is driven to a beekeeper's yard for relocation, and no poison touches your soil, crop or water.

What we put back afterward

Once the cavity is empty it gets closed: screen over pump house vents, mortar in block gaps, a snug new lid on a cracked valve or meter box, fresh siding or trim on a packing shed. Masonry, siding and trim repair work stays in-house: the licensed contractors, roofers and painters doing it are our own, and one outfit answers for the whole job. For a grove tree, we agree with you on how the cavity gets closed before any saw comes out, since the tree and its crop are yours. The workmanship is covered by our warranty, and a colony that finds its way back into a spot we sealed means another visit from us.

What moves the price on this job

A nest under a potting bench, in a stack of empty pots or in an irrigation box you can open standing up is ground-level work, usually falling between $300 and $400. A nest built into a block pump house wall, high in a grove tree, or buried in equipment that must be partly dismantled costs more, and a job that ends in repairs can run into the thousands. Quoting is free. Weekday visits are the base rate; night and weekend emergencies run higher. If your bookkeeper wants paperwork, the job can come with photos and an itemized invoice. South Dade farm country, block by block

  • Redland

    Redland is an unincorporated farm area named for pockets of red clay over oolitic limestone, with a large concentration of ornamental nurseries and tropical fruit groves, so shade houses, pump houses and mature fruit trees are part of the working landscape there.

  • Homestead

    Homestead, incorporated in 1913, is both a suburb and a major agricultural area. Because farm fields and suburban streets share the same city, a defensive colony at a field edge can matter to neighbors as well as your crew.

  • Florida City

    Florida City adjoins Homestead to the south and west and grew up on winter truck crops such as tomatoes and squash. Field equipment and irrigation that sit idle between seasons are just the kind of quiet cavity a swarm looks for.

Questions people ask us

Will you take the hives our beekeeper set out for pollination?

No. A rented pollination colony is someone else's property, and its owner is on record with the state. If those boxes are causing trouble, the person to call is that beekeeper. We handle feral colonies that moved into your structures, trees or equipment on their own.

Can my crew keep working the next block while you remove the colony?

The rest of the farm can usually keep going, provided engines and foot traffic stay outside the perimeter agreed on that morning. A colony that is already stinging people gets a wider buffer, since nobody can tell an Africanized colony from a European one by looking. On site, we point out exactly where work can continue.

Is it legal for someone to just kill a colony on my farm?

According to the state agriculture department, eradication is reserved for pest control companies holding a state license, while beekeepers doing live removal must be registered with the department. The property owner picks the method. Ours is live removal only, so the bees end up with a beekeeper.

There's a colony in an old avocado tree. Will we lose the tree?

Not as a rule. A thermal scan locates the brood nest inside the trunk, and the opening is placed to reach that and nothing more. We go over the cut and how to close the cavity with you before starting, and the tree colony page covers more.

Can you send insurance paperwork to our farm office or landlord?

Yes. Our crews are licensed and insured. Proof of coverage in the form of a COI can be emailed to your office, your landlord or a property manager ahead of the visit, the same paperwork commercial properties ask for. Tell us who needs to be named on it when you call.

Our packing shed runs in shifts. Can the removal fit around them?

We can schedule the visit for a gap between shifts or a slower day, and the 24-hour response guarantee still applies. Because after-dark and weekend emergency work is billed higher, a planned weekday slot tends to be the better value if nobody is being stung.
