Quick answer

Dial 911 ahead of anyone else if a person is struggling to breathe, puffing up around the mouth or neck, or covered in stings. After that, put a shut door or a rolled-up car window between your family, your pets and the bees, keep clear of the hole they are flying from, and leave sprays and hoses where they are. Somebody picks up our line day or night, anyone under attack jumps the queue, and a 24-hour response guarantee applies regardless of the hour. Expect a weekday visit to cost less than one booked for a Sunday or the middle of the night.

If anyone is being stung

Being stung now? Get inside a building or a closed car, covering your nose and mouth as you go. Dial 911 if anyone wheezes, feels faint, swells around the eyes, lips or throat, or has been stung many times. Leave the pool, the hose and the spray can alone. Once everyone is behind a shut door, call us.

Signs this is an emergency, not a scheduling call

  • Bees are chasing someone across the yard, bumping into faces and following people to the door instead of drifting off.
  • Dozens of bees are loose inside a bedroom, kitchen or office, crowding a window pane or circling a lamp.
  • A mower, hedge trimmer or pressure washer hit a hidden nest and the air around it suddenly filled with bees.
  • A child or a dog has been stung several times and the bees are still hanging around the spot where it happened.
  • Bees are streaming in and out of a gap beside a front door, a pool gate or a walkway that people cannot avoid.

What is going on behind it

A colony defends a zone around its nest, not just the nest itself. Vibration from a mower, a knock on the stucco or someone standing in the flight path reads as an attack. A stinging bee releases an alarm scent that smells faintly of banana, and it marks the person it hit, so more guards home in on that target. That is why moving away fast beats standing still and swatting. Bees inside a room usually come through a gap behind a light fixture, an outlet, a vent or a damaged ceiling, sent in by a colony living in the wall or attic; they head for daylight and lamps and cannot find their way back out. Africanized honey bees have reached Florida, and they can defend in far larger numbers and chase much farther than gentler stock. Nobody can tell the two apart by eye.

The next ten minutes, step by step

Work down this list in order. Each step buys distance or time.

  • Call 911 first for anyone with wheezing, a tight throat, swollen lips or tongue, dizziness, or a large number of stings. Use an epinephrine auto-injector if one has been prescribed.
  • Head for the nearest door you can shut, or climb into a vehicle and roll every window up. Pull a shirt over your nose and mouth on the way; bees aim for the face and airways.
  • Stay out of the pool. A defensive colony will circle the surface of a pool for as long as you hold your breath.
  • Once a door is between you and the colony, remain on that side of it. A few bees will follow you in, so move to another room and close that door too.
  • Scrape stingers off with a fingernail or the edge of a card as soon as you can. The venom sac keeps pumping after the bee is gone, so speed matters more than technique.
  • Bring dogs and cats inside and keep children away from windows that face the nest.
  • Call us, and text a photo of the opening if you can take one safely through glass.

Bees loose inside a room

Close the door to that room and push a rolled towel along the gap at the bottom. In daylight, open the blinds on one window in that room and switch off its lights; trapped bees crawl toward the brightest glass and stay there instead of spreading through the house. At night, keep the room dark and the hallway lights low so they are not drawn to the glow under the door. Skip the bug spray. It kills the bees you can see and does nothing to the colony feeding them in through the wall, and poisoned comb left in a cavity rots, leaks honey and attracts a fresh swarm later. The bees indoors are a symptom. Something in the wall, ceiling or attic is sending them in, and locating it is the real job. A thermal camera reads the warm cluster through drywall or plaster, so the opening goes where the nest actually is. See hives inside block and frame walls and what a thermal camera shows.

How an emergency job is handled on site

  1. Make the area safe

    The first move is clearing people, pets and cars out of the flight path and taping off a perimeter, so nobody wanders into the defense zone while the work goes on.

  2. Settle the bees

    A smoker and full protective gear let the technician work right at the entrance. Defensive bees calm down once the disturbance stops, so the pace stays slow and steady.

  3. Find the whole colony

    Thermal imaging traces the cluster and comb through stucco, block or drywall, so the cut-out follows where the nest sits rather than where the bees happened to be spotted.

  4. Take bees and comb out alive

    A soft-suction bee vac lifts the workers off the comb, brood sections are tied into frames, and the queen, workers and comb all leave for a beekeeper's yard. No pesticide is used.

  5. Clear the honey and close up

    Every scrap of honey and wax comes out of the cavity, because leftovers pull in new scouts. The entry point is then closed and the opening repaired.

What we put back afterward

Once the bees and comb are gone, both the opening we cut and the gap the bees used get closed. Patching stucco, resetting tile or hanging fresh drywall stays with our own crew; the roofer, the painter and the licensed contractor all work for us. You are not left holding a hole and a list of numbers to call. Honey that soaked into insulation or framing comes out along with the comb; see comb and honey cleanup. The workmanship carries a warranty, which means bees showing up again at a sealed spot get another visit from us. Photos of each stage and an itemized invoice are yours on request, handy for an insurance claim or a condo board file.

What moves the price on this job

Two things set the price on an emergency call: the hour and where the colony lives. Weekday daylight work is the baseline; an overnight or weekend trip is billed above it. Beyond that, a ground-level nest in a hedge, a meter box or a low wall usually runs about $300 to $400. A colony high in a roof or deep in a block wall can reach the thousands when lifts, cut-outs and rebuilding enter the picture. The quote is free, and you hear it before anyone starts cutting.

  • When the truck rolls: overnight and weekend emergency trips are priced above the weekday baseline.
  • Height and access: ladders, lifts or roof work add labor and time.
  • Where the comb sits: an open branch, a utility box, a wall cavity or a roof deck.
  • Repairs afterward: stucco, tile, drywall or paint finished by our own crews.

Questions people ask us

Should I call 911 or a bee removal company first?

911 first whenever someone has symptoms beyond local pain and swelling: wheezing, a tight throat, hives spreading across the body, vomiting, confusion or fainting. A large number of stings also calls for medical care, even in someone with no known allergy. Once the person is safe and help is on the way, call us so the bees are handled before anyone else walks into them.

Is it safe to wait until morning for bees inside the house?

Often, if the bees are shut in one room. Honey bees rarely fly in the dark, so traffic in and out of a wall colony largely stops at night. Close the room, block the door gap and keep the lights off near the bees. Call anyway: a real person picks up overnight, and together we can decide whether it needs a night visit or can wait for daylight.

Will a can of bug spray stop bees that are stinging right now?

Spraying a defensive colony tends to make the next few minutes worse. Bees that are not killed outright stay agitated, and the poison never reaches the queen or the comb inside a cavity. It also ends any chance of relocation, and dead comb in a wall rots and draws new bees, as our note on why bees come back explains. Stay indoors and let the colony be taken out alive.

Are these the Africanized kind, and does it matter?

You cannot tell by looking, and a technician cannot either without lab testing. Behavior is what matters. Bees that pour out at a light touch, chase people well past the property line or keep circling after you go inside should be treated as highly defensive, whatever their genetics turn out to be.

Does a call at 2 a.m. or on a Sunday cost more?

Yes. After dark and on weekends the rate goes up compared with an ordinary weekday. You hear the free quote up front, so you can weigh whether the job needs to happen now or can safely wait for a weekday. If nobody is in danger and the bees are contained, waiting is a reasonable choice.

What if bees came pouring out after a storm or a fallen tree?

A storm that rips open a soffit, cracks a roof or drops a hollow trunk can leave comb exposed and bees agitated. Keep well back from the break and call. Storm-exposed colonies get their own field guide article, and palm and tree colony removal covers downed trunks.
