Bees in the Soffit or Eave: Miami-Dade Hive Removal

The underside of your roof overhang is a long, dry, shaded box with a doorway at the bottom. Scout bees notice that. Here is how a colony gets in, how it comes out alive, and how the eave gets closed tight.

Quick answer

Bees streaming into a single gap under the overhang almost always mean a colony has built comb inside the soffit, the hollow space boxed in by the fascia board, the wall and the underside of the roof. Insecticide in that gap ends the traffic, yet comb, honey and dead brood stay boxed up to ferment. The fix is to open the soffit from below, take the bees and comb out alive, clean the cavity, then close it with sound panels and trim. There is no charge for the quote; a picture of the gap and the trim around it gets things moving.

If anyone is being stung

Heavy flying from the overhang, or a person stung several times, means people and pets go inside right away and the windows on that side of the roof edge get shut. If a sting victim feels faint, breaks out in hives all over, or has a tight chest, dial 911 first. Stay off the ladder, then call. Signs under the overhang

  • Bees flying in a tight stream to one corner of the eave, often where the fascia meets the wall or where two soffit panels butt together.
  • A low, constant hum you can hear from a ladder, or from a second-floor window that sits just above the overhang.
  • Dark, greasy streaks spreading across a soffit panel or running down the fascia, which can be wax and honey soaking through from the inside.
  • Bees slipping out of a dented aluminum panel, a channel that has pulled away from the wall, or a seam where a painted wood board has shrunk or rotted.
  • A ball of bees hanging under the eave for a day or two, then vanishing into the overhang instead of flying off.
  • Dead bees and flakes of pale wax collecting on a window sill, the patio or the driveway beneath one stretch of roof edge.

What is going on behind it

The eave is the part of the roof that hangs out past the wall. The fascia is the vertical board nailed across the ends of the rafters, and the soffit is the flat panel underneath, fastened to short lookout rafters. Together they enclose a hollow channel that rain rarely reaches, that stays shaded all day, and that already has a small opening near the bottom. That matches what a swarm's scouts search for: a sheltered cavity of roughly 40 liters with a low, narrow entrance. Once the swarm moves in, workers hang sheets of comb from the roof deck and rafters, raise brood in the middle and pack honey around the edges. Left alone, the comb keeps growing along the bay, and it can reach into the attic or the top of the wall.

How each kind of soffit lets bees in

Vented aluminum soffit snaps into channels along the fascia and the wall. When a channel works loose, or a ladder or a storm bends a panel, a slot opens that is plenty for a worker bee, since honey bees fit through gaps narrower than a pencil.

Wood soffits are plywood sheets or tongue-and-groove boards. As the paint fails, the edges swell and soften, and a seam opens where the board meets the fascia or the wall. Stucco soffits crack, and the screens behind their vent openings rust through.

Fascia gaps are their own problem. A gutter that sags can pull the fascia away from the roof deck and leave a long dark slit just under the shingles or tile.

Then there are eave returns, the short boxed sections where the overhang wraps around the corner of a gable. They are built from small pieces of trim with mitered joints, and every joint is a possible door. Because returns are small and closed, comb inside them fills the space quickly and is easy to miss from the ground. A colony that started in the soffit can also run up under the tile or flat roof or down into the wall cavity below.

Spraying the soffit gap: what stays behind afterward

The flying may stop a day or so after a dose of insecticide goes into the gap. Everything the colony made is still up there, though: sheets of wax, stored honey, pollen and dead brood, sealed inside the boxed overhang. In a closed soffit through a hot Miami-Dade summer, wax softens and honey seeps through panel seams and stains the paint below. Ants, roaches and wax moths move in to feed on what is left.

The smell of old comb also tells passing scouts that the cavity once held a colony, which is a big part of why an emptied cavity draws bees again. A sprayed eave can draw a new swarm the following season.

Caulking the entrance shut while bees are still inside has its own risk. The colony looks for another way out, and that path can lead through the attic and into the house. The order that works is bees out, comb out, cavity cleaned, then the seal. Our comb and honey cleanup covers that middle step.

Ladders, lifts and second-story overhangs

A one-story eave over a lawn is usually ladder work, but the ladder has to stand where a disturbed colony is not flying straight into the person on it. Two-story overhangs, eaves over a pool deck or a screened enclosure, and roof edges above dense hedges can call for a lift, so that someone can stand level under the comb with both hands free.

Whoever works the eave wears a full bee suit, and everyone else should stay indoors with pets inside and windows on that side shut until the panels are back up. On a condo or townhouse building, the board or manager usually has to approve access and wants paperwork first. A COI naming the association can be sent ahead of the visit, and our pages on condo and high-rise buildings and HOA and commercial properties explain how those visits are set up.

How an eave colony comes out

  1. Scan the overhang for warmth

    From the ground, the cluster and brood show up through the soffit panels as a warm patch on a thermal image, so only the bays holding comb get opened instead of the whole overhang.

  2. Set up safe access

    The ladder or lift goes into place, the ground below is roped off, and children, pets and open windows near the entrance are taken care of before any panel moves.

  3. Open the soffit from underneath

    Aluminum panels are slid out of their channels. Wood or stucco is sawn between rafters, so each patch has framing to fasten to and the fascia can stay put.

  4. Take the bees and the comb

    Bees are drawn off with a gentle low-suction vacuum, then the comb is cut free in pieces. Brood comb is kept for the beekeeper who takes the colony, and the queen goes with her workers.

  5. Scrape, clean and dry

    Every scrap of wax comes off the rafters and roof deck, honey is wiped out, and the cavity is left to dry before anything is closed. Photos of each stage are available on request.

What we put back afterward

Our own licensed tradespeople close the eave: a contractor for the framing, a roofer where the edge meets tile or shingle, a painter for the finish. Rotted fascia gets replaced, bent aluminum panels and loose channels are swapped or refastened, and wood soffit is patched on solid framing and painted to match. Vents stay open so the attic can breathe; insect screen goes behind them instead of sealant across them. Joints at the eave returns are tightened and sealed. The finish work is warrantied, so if bees later move into that same patched bay, someone comes back out. The repair page lists what else can be rebuilt.

What moves the price on this job

Work done from the ground sits at the low end, about $300-$400. An eave sits higher and hides more, so the total climbs from there with height, the length of comb inside, and how much of the overhang has to be rebuilt. A second-story job that needs a lift, comb that has spread into the attic, and new fascia and paint can bring the bill into the thousands. Questions people ask us

Can I just caulk the hole in my soffit shut?

Not while the bees are inside. Sealing the entrance traps the colony, and it will hunt for another exit, sometimes through the attic and into the house. Even if they die, the comb and honey stay in the cavity to rot and attract pests. Close the gap only after the bees and every piece of comb are out.

Will my whole soffit have to come down?

The aim is to open only the bays that hold comb. A thermal camera shows where the colony sits before anything is cut, so the opening is planned around the comb and the framing. If the comb has spread far along the eave, more panels come down, and the quote will say so up front.

Are the bees from my eave killed?

No. Nothing is sprayed into the soffit. The colony leaves your roof edge alive, brood comb and all, and is handed over to a beekeeper. Our page on relocation to beekeepers explains where the colony ends up after it leaves your roof edge.

My soffit has vents. Do they stay open after the repair?

Yes. Soffit vents let air move through the attic and help keep moisture down, so sealing them shut would cause a new problem. The usual fix is fine insect screen fastened behind the vent openings, which keeps bees out while the air still flows.

The bees are clustered on the outside of the eave. Is that a hive?

Bees bunched on the face of the fascia or soffit are usually a swarm resting between homes while its scouts pick a cavity, and swarms seldom stay at a stop like that more than about three days. If bees fly into a gap and you see no cluster, the colony is probably already inside. Read about scout bees or our swarm pickup page.
