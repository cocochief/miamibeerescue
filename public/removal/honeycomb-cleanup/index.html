Leftover comb and honey

Honeycomb removal in Miami-Dade when the bees are already gone

The buzzing stopped, but the wall still smells sweet and a brown streak is creeping down the paint. What stays behind after a colony dies or leaves keeps working on your house until someone takes it out.

Quick answer

Dead bees are only half the problem; the nest they built still has to be removed. Without workers guarding it, honey ferments, wax softens in summer heat, and wax moth and small hive beetle larvae move in. The result is odor, sticky stains that bleed through paint, and grubs crawling into rooms. Cleanup means opening the wall, ceiling or roof section, cutting out every piece of comb, scraping and drying the cavity, then sealing the entry so a new swarm can't follow the scent back in.

What a dead or empty hive tends to leave you with

  • A dark, greasy patch spreading on drywall, stucco or a ceiling, often worse on the hottest afternoons.
  • A sour, fermented smell near an outlet, vent or baseboard, sometimes compared to rotting fruit.
  • Small white grubs or tan worms dropping from a light fixture or wriggling along the floor near the old entry.
  • Bees from somewhere else flying in and out of a hole that went quiet weeks ago.
  • Ants or roaches lining up at a crack in the wall where the colony used to live.
  • Fine silk webbing and gritty dark specks around a soffit gap or attic vent.

What is going on behind it

A honey bee nest is mostly stored food: sheets of wax holding honey, pollen and developing brood. Living workers keep that pantry cool, clean and patrolled. Once they die, from spray or starvation, or once they abandon the site, nobody is on guard. Brood rots. Honey absorbs moisture and starts to ferment. Wax grows soft in a hot wall with no workers fanning to cool it, and heavy comb can sag, tear and let honey run down the inside of the wall. Two scavengers then take over: wax moth larvae that tunnel through the comb under silk, and small hive beetle larvae whose feeding turns honey frothy and foul. Grown beetle larvae wander off looking for soil to pupate in, and inside a house that search can lead them into your living room.

Why spraying the bees only finished half the job

A dead colony is not a gone colony. Poison ends the stinging, yet the nest itself, wax, honey and rotting brood, stays exactly where the bees built it. The University of Florida's consumer guide on bee removal says an unwanted colony is the property owner's to deal with, and warns that a pest control operator may leave the comb in place, or never explain why it matters, even when hired to clear the whole nest. Within several days, larvae feeding on the leftover comb can find their way indoors, and honey can drip through next to the old nest. If poison was used, treat any honey you find as contaminated and never taste it or feed it to pets. The cleanup that should have followed is what this page covers. If some bees survived the spray, the live part goes to a live removal before we start cutting.

Colonies that left on their own

Sometimes nobody touched the bees. A colony can die out over a bad stretch, or abscond, leaving as a whole when pests or disturbance overwhelm it. The comb stays where it was built. That empty, scented cavity is a standing invitation: scout bees from a passing swarm recognize old comb as a ready-made home, which is exactly why beekeepers bait swarm boxes with it. If you have watched new bees take over an old spot, our guide on repeat colonies explains the pull. If the house is empty for a season, a part-time residence can sit with fermenting comb for months before anyone notices.

Miami-Dade heat speeds everything up

Miami sits in a tropical monsoon climate, with a hot, wet season from May to October and summer temperatures in the mid-80s to low 90s. That range overlaps the 84 to 91 degree band in which greater wax moth larvae develop best, and an attic or a sun-facing wall runs hotter still. Humidity gives honey more water to pull in, which helps fermentation along. Small hive beetles were confirmed in a Florida apiary in 1998, and yeasts associated with their larvae spoil honey quickly. Comb left in a wall through a Miami-Dade summer has heat and moisture working on it the whole time, with nothing to slow either one down.

Stains, smells and selling the house

Honey and melted wax soak into drywall, plaster and wood, and the stain they leave will bleed through ordinary paint and lift wallpaper. Scrubbing the outside of the wall does nothing while the source is still inside. Once comb is out, saturated board usually has to be cut away and replaced, and the cavity needs to dry before anything gets closed up. If a buyer's inspector has flagged an old hive, see bees found during a home sale; renters and owners sorting out who pays can start with our tenant and landlord guide.

How the comb comes out

  1. Check that nothing is alive

    Before any cutting, a thermal camera goes over the area. A surviving cluster or a fresh swarm shows as a warm patch, and any live bees are vacuumed out for relocation before cutting begins.

  2. Open the right section

    Using the stain pattern, the entry point and the thermal image, we open the smallest surface that exposes the whole nest: a drywall panel, a few roof tiles, a soffit board or a stucco cut.

  3. Cut and scrape every piece

    All comb comes out by hand, including the thin wax bases bees glue to studs, joists and block. Honey-soaked insulation goes with it. Leaving scraps behind feeds the next round of larvae.

  4. Clean and dry the cavity

    Pooled honey is wiped and washed out, beetle and moth larvae are removed, and the space is left to dry so no moisture or residue stays trapped behind the new surface.

  5. Seal the way in

    The original entry, and any gap close enough to serve as a substitute, is closed with materials that suit the wall or roof, so the empty cavity stops advertising itself to scouts.

What we put back afterward

The hole we made gets closed by people who answer to us: our own licensed contractors patch the board or stucco, our roofers reset tile, and our painters finish the surface, so one call covers it. Stained areas get a stain-blocking primer before finish paint so the brown shadow is less likely to bleed back. That closing work is warrantied, so if a later swarm gets into the same sealed cavity, we return to deal with it. Ask and you get photos of the open cavity and an itemized invoice, which helps with an insurance claim or a property file. Details on the follow-up work are on repairs after removal.

What moves the price on this job

What you pay for comb cleanup tracks the size of the opening and the rebuild, not the bee count. A small nest that can be reached from the ground and closed with a simple patch usually lands around $300 to $400. A large, long-dead nest inside a second-story wall or under a tile roof, with soaked drywall and finish work, can run into the thousands. Comb rarely needs a midnight crew, and choosing a weekday slot over a night or weekend call keeps the bill lower. Quotes cost nothing, and pricing in detail lays out the ranges.

  • How much comb is inside and how long it has been rotting
  • Where the nest sits: low on a first-floor wall, up a ladder, or under roof tile
  • What has to be cut open: drywall, stucco, tile, soffit or wood siding
  • How much stained or honey-soaked material must be replaced
  • Whether paint, texture or roof tile has to be matched afterward

Questions people ask us

The exterminator already killed the bees. Do I really need the comb taken out?

Yes. Dead bees stop stinging, but the honey, wax and brood stay behind. Unguarded, they ferment, attract ants, roaches, wax moths and beetles, and can drip or stain through the wall. Removing the nest is the only way to stop those problems for good.

Can I just seal the hole and leave the comb inside?

That traps the mess with its food supply. The honey still ferments and stains, larvae still look for a way out, and they may come out inside the house instead. Seal only after the cavity is empty, clean and dry.

Is the honey from a dead colony safe to keep?

Not if the bees were sprayed, because the pesticide may be in it. Even without poison, honey that small hive beetle larvae have worked through ferments and is considered unfit to eat. Plan on throwing it all away.

How can you tell where the old nest is if no bees are flying?

Stain shape, the old entry, sound when the wall is tapped, and the thermal camera together point to the spot. Thermal imaging also helps show whether anything alive is left, which is covered in our thermal imaging guide.

Will new bees move into the same wall later?

They can, because old comb and wax residue smell like a proven home. That is why every scrap is scraped out and the entry is sealed. If bees do return to a spot we sealed, we come back.
