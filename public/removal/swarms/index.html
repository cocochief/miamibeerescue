Bee Swarm Removal in Miami: Resting Cluster or New Hive?

A ball of bees on your fence or side mirror is a colony between homes. Plenty move on by themselves. Some choose your soffit instead. Learning the difference tells you whether to wait or pick up the phone.

Quick answer

A swarm is a queen and thousands of her workers resting in the open while scouts hunt for a cavity. Out on a branch or rail, the cluster is calm and can lift off after a few hours or a few days. Give it room, bring the dog inside, and resist reaching for the spray can. If you see bees streaming into a gap in a wall, roof or meter box, call right away: that is a colony settling in, and each day it stays means more comb to cut out later.

If anyone is being stung

If bees are stinging someone, get that person into a house or a car right away, moving in a straight line and covering the face. Don't jump into a pool or hide in bushes, and don't swat. Dial 911 when someone is wheezing, swelling around the face, or has been stung many times. Once everyone is safe indoors, ring us, because a sting call jumps the queue at any hour.

What you're probably looking at

  • A dense, hanging mass of bees, football-sized or larger, on a branch, hedge, fence rail or patio chair.
  • A loud cloud of bees swirling over the yard, then funneling down onto a single spot until the air clears.
  • A cluster on a car's bumper, side mirror or wheel well, often on a vehicle that has sat parked for a day or two.
  • Bees bunched on a balcony railing, under a patio table or against storm shutters several floors up.
  • A steady line of bees flying into a crack, vent or weep hole near where the cluster was hanging.

What is going on behind it

Swarming is how a honey bee colony reproduces. The founding queen departs, taking roughly 30 to 60 percent of the workforce along, while the bees that stay home raise a replacement. The departing group can't fly far with her, so it bivouacs on the first handy perch: your ficus, your fence, your car. From that cluster, a few dozen scout bees head out to inspect possible homes and report back by dancing on the surface of the cluster. Once enough scouts agree on one site, the whole mass takes off together. Until then the bees have no comb, no brood and no stored honey to protect, which is why a resting swarm is usually so placid.

Why most swarms leave, and why some stay

A swarm in the open is on a clock. It carries only the honey the workers loaded before leaving, so the scouts need a decision fast, and a cluster that sits more than three days at one stop is unusual. When the bees vanish overnight, the scouts found somewhere to live. The question for you is where.

Sometimes the answer is close by. The cavity they chose may be the wall, attic or roof of the house the cluster was hanging beside, and the move can be quick once they decide. Other times a swarm finds nothing suitable and starts building wax right where it is. If you notice pale comb forming under the branch or rail, the bees are no longer passing through. Florida extension guidance notes that African honey bees have been known to nest in the open near people, and they can't be told apart from gentler honey bees without lab testing. Treat any colony that settles as one to have handled, not watched.

The urgent moment: bees going into a cavity

Scouts favor an enclosed space of roughly 40 liters, with a small entrance near the bottom. A block wall void, the gap behind a fascia board, a hollow under roof tile, an irrigation valve box or an empty electrical cabinet all fit that profile. When you see bees walking into a hole in a steady stream, the colony is moving in, and workers begin drawing comb almost immediately.

This is when timing matters most. A swarm that entered the wall cavity this morning has little comb and no brood yet, so taking it out means a smaller opening and less cleanup. Wait a few weeks and you are dealing with sheets of comb, stored honey and developing bees, plus a colony that now has something to defend. Call as soon as you see the line of bees going in, even if most of the cluster still looks like it is resting outside. The same applies to a soffit or eave gap or a meter or valve box.

Cars, balconies and fences: awkward landing spots

A cluster on a car tempts people to simply drive off. Please don't. The bees cling on, then pour off at the first stop, often next to a stranger, and the queen may end up lost somewhere in the engine bay or under the trim. Park it where it is, roll the windows up, and let us see a picture first.

Balconies bring a different problem: the cluster may be on your railing while the neighbors' sliding doors open a few feet away. In a tower, the association or manager usually needs to know, and our condo and high-rise work includes insurance certificates for the building's file. Fences and gates are the easiest pickups, as long as nobody tries to knock the cluster loose first.

What to avoid while you wait

Spraying a swarm with a garden hose, bug spray or soapy water breaks the cluster into thousands of flying, agitated bees with nowhere to go, and some will find a gap in the house. Smoke and fire carry the same risk, with a fire hazard on top. Mowers, hedge trimmers and leaf blowers near the cluster send vibration straight into it.

Instead, bring children and pets indoors, close nearby windows and doors, and watch from inside. If the cluster sits near a walkway or a pool deck, keep that area clear until the bees are gone. How a swarm pickup works

  1. Photo and a few questions

    Your photo shows the size of the cluster, how high it sits, and whether bees are already flying into a gap nearby. That decides whether this is a simple pickup or a cavity job.

  2. Box goes under the cluster

    A ventilated swarm box is placed right beneath the bees. On a branch, one firm shake drops most of them in. On a fence, a car or a railing, they are brushed and scooped gently.

  3. Stragglers follow the queen

    The lid stays cracked while flying bees home in on the queen's scent. Bees fanning at the entrance with tails raised are a good sign that she is inside the box.

  4. Checking where they landed

    If bees were seen entering the house, nearby walls, soffits and vents get a thermal imaging scan, so a second group isn't left behind in a cavity.

  5. A ride to a beekeeper

    The closed box goes to a beekeeper who settles the swarm into a hive. Nothing is sprayed and no bees are killed.

What we put back afterward

A swarm that only rested on a branch or fence leaves nothing to fix. At most there is a little scent and wax residue, which is worth wiping away because it can draw later swarms to the same spot; our guide on why bees return covers that. If the bees had already moved into a cavity, it is a different job: the comb is cut out, then licensed contractors, roofers and painters from our own crew patch the opening as part of repairs after removal. That work is warrantied. Should a swarm ever turn up again at an entry we closed, we return to deal with it.

Questions people ask us

Will the swarm on my tree leave by itself?

Often, yes. A resting swarm is waiting for its scouts to agree on a new home, and that can take a few hours or a few days. Trouble starts when the scouts vote for a hollow in your own attic or wall. If you need the bees gone sooner, or you notice them entering the house, ask for a pickup instead of waiting.

Is it safe to walk past a swarm cluster?

A cluster at rest is between homes and has little reason to sting. Still, keep a wide berth, walk slowly, and keep kids, dogs and lawn equipment away. In Florida you cannot tell African honey bees from other honey bees by looking, so don't test the cluster's patience.

There's a swarm stuck to my car's mirror. Should I drive it somewhere?

Please don't. The bees hang on, then fly off at your next stop, and some may crawl into vents or the engine bay. Leave the car parked with the windows closed and send a photo. Bees on a bumper or mirror can usually be brushed gently into a swarm box.

Why did the swarm choose my house?

Scouts look for a dry cavity of the right size with a small entrance, and many houses offer one in a wall, soffit or roof. Old colony scent can also attract new swarms. Our repeat-visit guide explains how sealing helps.

Do the bees get killed?

No. Every swarm goes alive to a beekeeper, who settles it into a hive box. Nothing is sprayed, and the bees keep their queen. You can read more about how colonies are relocated after they leave your yard, and what the beekeeper does with them next.

Who should call about a swarm on a condo balcony or common area?

Either the resident or the manager can call. For common areas, the association usually wants a certificate of insurance before work starts, and we can send one. For board minutes, just ask and we'll include photos and an itemized invoice.
