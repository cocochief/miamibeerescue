Bee Relocation in Miami-Dade: What Happens After the Colony Leaves Your House

A colony pulled from your soffit is not saved yet. What decides whether it lives comes afterward: the evening drive, the hand-off and how the colony settles at the apiary.

Quick answer

Relocation means the colony comes out alive, comb and all, and moves to a beekeeper's apiary far enough from your property that the foragers cannot find their way back. Nothing is sprayed. Young brood rides in wooden frames beside its nurse bees, the queen stays with her daughters, and the lid goes on for transport only after the field bees have come home. Florida's agriculture department tells homeowners to ask any removal company where the bees will end up. Ask us that question, and you will get a plain answer: a beekeeper's yard.

If anyone is being stung

Someone under attack needs walls or a car door between them and the bees, so move indoors and close up; skip the pool, as bees wait above it for you. Once inside, push stingers out with a fingernail; pinching squeezes in more venom. Dial 911 for a swollen throat, wheezing or dozens of stings. What is going on behind it

A honey bee colony is one working unit: a single queen, thousands of workers and a nest of wax comb holding eggs, larvae, pollen and honey. It survives a move only if those parts travel together and land somewhere with space, forage and a person to manage it. Spraying sidesteps that by killing the colony in place, yet the comb stays behind. Unguarded honey draws robber bees from other hives, the scent invites the next swarm into the same hollow, and dead brood rots against your drywall. Relocation takes the whole nest out, which removes the attraction along with the insects, and it keeps a productive colony at work making honey or pollinating crops. The scent trail is covered in our field guide piece on repeat colonies.

From the cavity to the hive box

With the wall or soffit opened, the nest is taken apart one wax panel at a time. Brood comb, the cells holding capped or growing young, is the colony's future, so it is trimmed to size and held inside empty wooden frames with rubber bands or string. Those frames go into the hive box in roughly the order they hung in the wall, since bees keep brood at the center of the nest and stores at the edges. Heavy honey comb is too soft to frame, so it is packed separately in sealed containers as part of comb and honey cleanup. Loose bees are gathered with a low-suction bee vacuum that drops them into a screened chamber without crushing them. The queen matters most. If she is spotted, she is caged and set in the box; if not, workers fanning at the box entrance with their tails raised are a fair sign she made it inside.

Why the box waits for dark

Foragers spend the day in the field, and any still out when the box is closed will return to an empty hole. So the box stays beside the original entrance until evening, when nearly every field bee has flown home and settled. Then the entrance is screened shut and the box is strapped down for the drive. Distance matters as much as timing. A worker memorizes the landmarks around her home, and a colony shifted only a few hundred yards loses its older bees back to your house. Taking it miles away forces every forager to reorient and learn the new site. Heat is the other risk. A closed box in a parked vehicle on a Miami afternoon can cook brood, which is one more reason the move happens in the cooler hours with ventilation screens in place.

Life in the beekeeper's yard

At the apiary the box goes onto a stand, the screen comes off and the colony gets a few days to calm down. The beekeeper then checks that the queen is laying, feeds the colony if its stores were lost in the move, and watches for small hive beetles and varroa mites, which hit a stressed colony hardest. Any colony in South Florida could carry African genetics, and the state agriculture department notes that only a genetic test can tell African and European bees apart. UF/IFAS extension material describes beekeepers in African bee areas requeening with European queens, and the colony's temper shifts over the following weeks as her daughters replace the old workers. Whatever the keeper decides, the bees are now managed instead of living unchecked inside a building, as they did in your block or frame wall.

What Florida law says about relocation

Florida treats bees as both a pest and part of agriculture, and the state rule that separates the two is specific. Under Rule 5E-14.151 of the Florida Administrative Code, removing and relocating bees from a structure, lawn or ornamental planting for honey production or crop pollination is not pest control when a beekeeper registered with the state does it. Removing bees to get rid of them as pests is pest control, and the agriculture department reserves eradication for pest control firms holding a state license. That split hands you a useful question for any company you call: where are the bees going? A clear answer names a beekeeper. If nobody can tell you, assume the plan is poison, and keep looking.

How a relocation job runs

  1. Find the whole nest

    A thermal camera reads the heat a living nest gives off, which lets the cut land over the comb itself instead of at the doorway the bees use. See what thermal imaging shows.

  2. Open up and frame comb

    The cavity is opened, brood comb is cut and fitted into frames, and honey comb is packed separately in sealed containers so it cannot drip into the wall or the box.

  3. Gather the bees and queen

    A gentle bee vacuum collects loose workers into a screened chamber, and the queen is caged whenever she can be found so the colony stays in one piece.

  4. Wait, screen, drive

    The box sits by the old entrance until foragers come home in the evening, then it is screened, strapped down and driven out to the beekeeper's apiary.

  5. Close the empty cavity

    Leftover wax is scraped out, the hollow is cleaned and the entry is sealed so the lingering scent does not pull a new swarm into the same space.

What we put back afterward

Moving the colony is wasted effort if the hollow stays open, because a cleaned-out wall still smells like a home to the next scout bees. After the comb comes out, the space is scraped to bare surfaces and honey residue is wiped away. The entry gap is closed, and any stucco, soffit, drywall or tile removed to reach the nest is rebuilt by licensed tradespeople on our own crew, including roofers and painters, as part of repairs after removal. A warranty stands behind that workmanship, so a colony settling again where we closed things up earns you a return trip. Ask for pictures and an itemized bill, and both come with the job.

What moves the price on this job

Bees reachable from the ground, like a hedge clump or a meter box nest, usually cost $300 to $400. Colonies built deep inside a wall, roof or ceiling cost more, sometimes into the thousands, because the price then covers opening the structure, saving the comb and repairing what was opened. Need someone out after dark, or on a Sunday? That urgency adds to the bill. Questions people ask us

Will the bees survive being moved to a new place?

A colony moved with its comb, its queen and most of its workers has a good chance, provided the box stays cool on the road. If the queen is lost, the workers can raise a new one from young larvae in the brood comb, which is why brood is framed instead of thrown out. The shakiest stretch is the first week at the apiary, when the beekeeper checks for laying, food stores and pests.

Why not set the colony down a few yards away, behind the garage?

Because they would be home again by lunch. Bees navigate by landmarks, so a colony shifted a short distance sends its older foragers straight back to the original entrance, where they cluster with nowhere to go. Moving the colony miles away resets their map. It also takes the bees away from a spot where people, pets and lawn equipment pass every day.

What happens to the honey and wax left from the nest?

Honey-filled panels are sealed in containers and hauled off with the colony instead of being left in your wall to ferment, stain and draw pests. Brood comb travels with the bees in their new frames. Any thin smear of wax left on the framing is scraped away before the cavity is sealed, because that scent is what calls the next swarm.

Could the bees moved off my property carry African genetics?

Nobody can tell by looking. Florida's agriculture department says a genetic test is the only way to separate African from European honey bees, so every colony here deserves caution whatever its mood. That is a good reason to leave a colony in a wall to people with suits and gear, and a reason a swarm on a branch should not be poked with a broom either.

How do I know my bees really went to a beekeeper?

Ask, and expect a specific answer. Florida's rules treat live removal for honey or pollination differently from pest control, and the state agriculture department advises consumers to ask where removed bees are taken. Photos from the job are available if you want them, and the itemized invoice lists the relocation next to any repair work.

Is relocation safer for kids and pets than spraying?

In one plain way, yes: no pesticide goes into your walls, yard or air. During the work, keep children and animals indoors and away from the opening, because disturbed bees defend their nest. Once the box is closed and gone, the area is ordinary again, with no dead colony left to rot or attract robbing bees from elsewhere.
