Field guide: repeat colonies

The Miami-Dade wall or box bees keep returning to

A colony leaves, or gets sprayed, and a few months later a new swarm is working the exact same crack. That is not bad luck. The spot itself is still advertising.

Quick answer

Bees keep coming back because the space that drew the first colony is still there, still open and often still lined with wax, honey and propolis. Scout bees from a passing swarm judge a cavity by its size, its entrance and whether old comb is waiting inside, and abandoned comb counts heavily in its favour. Ending the cycle takes three things: every scrap of comb scraped out, the empty void filled, and the entrance closed with a material bees cannot chew or squeeze past.

The spot did the choosing

It is easy to think of the bees as the problem. From the bees' side, though, your wall bay or meter box was the winner of a contest. When a colony swarms, a small group of scouts fans out and inspects possible homes, then the cluster settles on the best one. A good home is a dry, enclosed space of roughly 40 litres with a small entrance low down, about two square inches. A hollow block cell, the gap between two studs, a soffit run or a buried irrigation valve box can all fit that description almost perfectly.

Taking the bees away does nothing to the space. As long as the void keeps its shape and the opening stays open, the next set of scouts will rate it just as highly as the last set did.

What old comb tells a scout

Building comb costs a colony a great deal of nectar, because worker bees have to eat honey to produce wax. A cavity that already holds comb lets a new swarm skip much of that work, so scouts favour sites where abandoned honeycomb is still in place.

That is why a half-finished removal is so risky. Comb left behind a wall still smells of wax, honey and propolis, the resin bees use to glue and seal their home. To a scout, that smell and that ready-made structure say the spot has already been tested and has worked.

Leftover comb causes other trouble too. Honey can ferment and seep through drywall or stain a ceiling, and the remains draw beetles, ants, cockroaches and flies. Full comb and honey cleanup is the part of the job that takes the welcome sign down.

A poisoned nest stays furnished for the next swarm

Killing a colony inside a wall leaves the whole nest behind: comb, stored honey, brood and thousands of dead bees. Nobody is left to fan the comb and keep it cool, so in a hot attic or a sun-facing wall the wax can soften and honey can run. Then the cavity sits there, already furnished, with its entrance untouched.

Sealing the hole right after spraying is just as bad. You trap the decay inside and any surviving bees push deeper, sometimes finding a way into the living space. A live removal works the other way around. The comb is cut out, the colony is handed to a beekeeper through colony relocation, and only then is the opening closed. With a colony inside a wall, that means opening the block or frame wall at the nest, not just at the hole bees use.

Gaps bees actually use

The doorway does not need to be big. Florida extension guidance treats any gap wider than about 1/8 inch as worth sealing, which covers far more of a house than most people expect.

The usual entry points are ordinary ones: a gap where a pipe or cable passes through stucco, a weep hole, a loose soffit vent, a missing roof tile, the space around an air conditioning line set, a cracked water meter lid or an open downspout. Outside the house, hollow fence posts, stacked lumber, old tires and unused containers all offer a dark room with a small door.

If the first colony used a particular gap, the scouts that come later will find it the same way. Closing it is the single change that matters most, and it only works once the space behind it is empty.

Seal, fill, then close

A proper close-up happens in layers. First the cavity is scraped clean of comb and propolis so nothing inside still smells like a hive. Next the void itself is filled with insulation or another packing material, so that even if a gap opens later there is no 40-litre room waiting behind it. Last comes the outer seal.

Material matters. Silicone caulk handles cracks, concrete crack filler suits block and masonry, and expanding foam works for larger holes as long as the exposed face is painted so sun and rain do not break it down. Vents, drains and downspouts that must keep breathing get 1/8 inch hardware cloth instead, which lets air and water pass but stops a bee.

If a wall or roof had to come open, the repairs after removal stay in house: licensed contractors, roofers and painters on our side rebuild the patch so it holds instead of being taped over.

Shutting the vacancy for good

  1. Never seal a hole while bees are still flying in and out of it.
  2. Ask whether every piece of comb was taken out, not just the bees.
  3. Fill the empty void with insulation or packing so no open room remains behind the wall.
  4. Seal cracks over 1/8 inch with silicone caulk, or crack filler on block and concrete.
  5. Anything that must keep passing air or water, like a dryer vent or gutter outlet, gets a fine metal mesh rather than caulk.
  6. Paint any foam left exposed outdoors, since bare foam cracks and erodes in the weather.
  7. Walk the outside of the house each week from March to July and after every storm.
  8. Clear out hollow fence posts, old tires, stacked lumber and empty containers in the yard.

Miami's calendar keeps the scouts busy

In a place with real winters, colonies sit out the cold months clustered tight, and swarming is squeezed into a short spring window. Miami's tropical monsoon climate rarely gets anywhere near freezing, so colonies stay active through the cooler months. University of Florida extension advice names March through July as swarming season and suggests checking your property weekly during those months.

Hurricane season, which runs from June 1 to November 30, adds its own risk. Wind can lift tiles, crack soffits and loosen vents, opening fresh doorways into spaces that used to be closed. After a storm, it is worth walking the outside of the house and reading our note on colonies exposed after a storm.

Africanized honey bees make up a large share of the feral colonies down here, which defend their nest more strongly. That is one more reason to keep openings closed instead of hoping the next swarm passes by.

How to tell new bees from old ones

Bees at a spot that was dealt with months ago are usually a new swarm, not the old colony returning. A handful of bees circling a seam and landing briefly are probably scouts; our guide to scout bees checking out the house covers what to do at that stage. Constant two-way traffic at one opening, with some foragers arriving with pollen packed on their hind legs, means a colony has already moved in.

If you are not sure whether anything is still alive behind a wall, thermal imaging can show the warm cluster from outside without cutting first. That helps answer the question of whether you are looking at a fresh arrival or comb that was never fully removed.

Questions people ask us

Is it the same colony coming back after it was removed?

Almost never. Once a colony has been taken out live and relocated, it is not coming back on its own. What you are seeing is nearly always a new swarm whose scouts found the same cavity attractive for the same reasons the first colony did, especially if comb or honey was left behind.

Can I just fill the hole with foam to keep bees out?

Only if you are certain the space behind it is empty. Foaming over a live colony traps the bees, who will push deeper and may find another way out, possibly into the house. With an empty cavity, foam is fine for bigger holes, provided the outside face gets a coat of paint.

Why did bees move in again after the exterminator sprayed?

Because the poison only dealt with the insects. Their comb, their stored honey and the doorway they used all survived the treatment, and a cavity with comb already built is exactly what a scout favours. That wall stays on the market until the comb is cut out, the void is packed and the gap is sealed.
