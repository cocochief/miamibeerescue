Field guide: buying and selling

Bees found during a home inspection in Miami-Dade

A line about bees on an inspection report can stall a sale or sour a negotiation. Here is how buyers and sellers in Miami-Dade get the colony out, on paper, before the closing date.

Why an inspector writes bees down

Florida law describes a home inspection as a limited visual examination of the readily accessible systems and components of a house: the structure, roof covering, exterior, plumbing, electrical, HVAC and site conditions that affect the structure. Inspectors don't cut into drywall or lift roof tiles, so a colony reaches the report as a sighting rather than a diagnosis, such as a short line about bee activity at a soffit corner or a gap in the stucco.

That line could mean a few foragers working a flowering hedge, a swarm resting for a day, or a colony that has been building comb inside a wall cavity for months. The report can't tell those apart, and neither can you from the driveway. What sorts them out is the traffic pattern. Bees flying straight into one opening and back out, steadily, all day, point to an established nest. A cluster hanging in the open on a branch or fence points to a swarm that may leave on its own. The guide to scout bees covers the earlier stage, when a handful of bees are only inspecting a gap.

The termite report is a different document

Buyers and lenders in Florida commonly order a wood-destroying organism inspection, the WDO or termite report, next to the general inspection. State statute defines a wood-destroying organism as arthropod or plant life that damages and can reinfest seasoned wood in a structure, and it names the list: termites, powder-post beetles, old house borers and wood-decaying fungi. Honey bees aren't on it. A WDO inspector who happens to notice a colony may mention it, but the form isn't built to locate one, and the statute says the report does not promise those organisms are absent unless it spells out otherwise.

So a clean termite report and a bee note from the home inspector can both be accurate at once. The harm a long-settled colony does is also different from termite damage: wax and honey soaking into drywall, moisture held around the comb, and stains that eventually show through a ceiling. Keep the WDO paperwork and the bee question as two separate items in your transaction file.

Selling: what you already know matters

Florida sellers can't lean on buyer beware. In Johnson v. Davis, the Florida Supreme Court held that a seller who knows of facts materially affecting the value of a home, facts that are not readily observable and not known to the buyer, has a duty to disclose them. A colony living behind a bathroom wall can fit that description. How it is worded on your disclosure is a question for your real estate attorney, and nothing on this page is legal advice.

Here is the practical side. Taking the bees out does not erase what you knew, but it changes what you are able to say. A note that a colony in the east wall was removed alive on a given date, the comb was taken out, the cavity sealed and the drywall patched is much easier for a buyer to accept than bees, status unknown. Ask for photos and an itemized invoice so the record backs up every part of that note. If the house is a rental, the tenant and landlord guide sorts out who should call and who should pay.

Buying: ask for live removal, not spray

When the bee note shows up in your inspection results, you have a few levers. You can ask the seller to have the colony removed and the damage repaired before closing, ask for a credit, or cancel if your contract terms allow it. Whichever you pick, put the method in writing. Spray or a pest treatment pushed through the entry hole kills the bees but leaves the comb behind, and in an attic that bakes all summer or a west-facing wall, that comb can soften, leak honey and pull ants, roaches and wax moths into the cavity. Then the smell and the stain become yours.

A live removal opens the cavity, lifts out the bees along with all of their wax, and sends the colony to a beekeeper. Name it in your repair request, along with comb and honey cleanup and repairs after removal, and ask that the photos and invoice reach you before the walkthrough. If the colony is inside block or frame walls, expect the repair to be the larger share of the work.

Fitting the job inside your closing window

Contract dates leave little slack, so the order of steps matters. First, call with the address and a copy of the inspector's note. Calls reach a person at 3 a.m. as readily as at noon, the 24-hour response guarantee starts with that call, and a caller who is being stung is helped before anything else. Second, the visit: the crew follows the flight path and uses thermal imaging to find where the warm cluster sits behind the surface, which tells both sides whether this is a small cut or a large opening. What thermal imaging shows explains that step. Third, the removal and the sealing, so scouts from the next swarm don't claim the empty space. Fourth, the patch. The roofers, painters and other licensed contractors who rebuild the stucco, drywall or tile work for us directly, so closing the surface does not mean booking a second company.

Book the first visit early in the inspection period. Emergency work after dark or on Saturday and Sunday carries a higher rate than a weekday slot, and there is no reason to pay that premium because the note sat in someone's inbox. Visits cover the whole county, from Aventura and Miami Gardens down to Homestead and out to the county line.

Before the final walkthrough

  1. Photograph the entry point the inspector flagged from a safe distance, and note the time of day.
  2. Keep pets, children and the buyer's contractors away from the opening until the bees are gone.
  3. Don't spray, plug or caulk the hole, since bees sealed inside may hunt for another exit and find one into the living space.
  4. Schedule the removal in the first days of the inspection period instead of the last.
  5. Ask for photos and an itemized invoice, then add both to the transaction file.
  6. Confirm the opening is sealed and the surface repaired before the buyer walks through.
  7. Let your attorney or agent decide how the completed removal is described on the disclosure.

Paperwork a buyer will actually read

You can ask for photos and an itemized invoice on any job, and during a sale they carry more weight than on an ordinary job. Useful photos show the opened cavity with comb still in it, the same cavity empty, and the finished patch. The invoice separates each part of the work: the removal, the comb and honey taken out, the sealing, and any carpentry, stucco, roofing or paint. A buyer's agent can see at a glance that the problem was handled at its source rather than sprayed and left.

File a copy with your closing documents. Workmanship is warrantied, which means a sealed opening that draws bees again gets a return visit, and an invoice naming the exact location makes that easy to confirm later. Who pays, and roughly how much

Who pays is part of your negotiation, but the figures themselves are plain. Small, reachable colonies cost the least: a ground-level job, such as bees in an irrigation valve box beside the driveway, sits around $300 to $400. Once a wall, a tile or flat roof or a soffit has to be opened and rebuilt, the total can reach the thousands with repairs included. The quote is free, which gives both sides a number to write into a repair addendum or a credit request, and the drivers behind a bigger or smaller bill are listed on our pricing page.

In a condo or townhouse community the colony may sit in a common area the association controls, and the board may ask for proof of insurance before anyone touches the building. The board can be sent COIs, and the page for associations and property managers walks through those arrangements.

Questions people ask us

Does a WDO or termite inspection in Florida check for bees?

No. The WDO report targets termites, wood-boring beetles and wood-decay fungi, the organisms named in Florida's statute. An inspector may note a colony if it is obvious, but the report isn't designed to find bees or estimate how much comb is inside a wall, so a clean report says nothing either way.

The colony is gone now. Does it still belong on my seller's disclosure?

Your real estate attorney should decide, because the answer turns on your facts. Under Florida's disclosure duty, a seller has to share known facts that materially affect value and aren't readily observable. A finished removal backed by photos and an itemized invoice is usually far easier to explain than an open question about bees in the wall.

Can the bees just be sprayed so we close on time?

A spray job gets you a dead colony, not a clean wall. Comb and honey stay inside the structure, and after the new owner moves in they can ferment, leak and draw pests. A live removal takes all of it out, sends the colony to a beekeeper, and ends with the opening sealed and repaired, which is the record a careful buyer will want.

We close in ten days. Is there still time?

There can be, if you call today. A ten-day closing is exactly the case the 24-hour response guarantee exists for, and the line is staffed day or night. The first look settles the scope. How long repairs take turns on the surface involved, and patching a soffit is quicker than re-laying roof tile.

Does the buyer or the seller need to be home for the removal?

Neither has to be there, as long as someone can provide access to the yard, attic or unit and the listing agent knows the work is scheduled. If the house is empty between owners, read our seasonal-home article for ways to arrange access while nobody lives there.
