Field guide: before the swarm

Scout bees around the house in Miami-Dade: what they are deciding

A handful of bees circling one gap in a soffit or block wall are usually not a colony yet. They are scouts, and the next day or two decides whether thousands follow.

Quick answer

Scout bees are surveyors sent out by a swarm that has left its old hive and is hanging somewhere nearby. They hover at one opening, walk in and out, and fly off without carrying pollen. If you see that at your house, keep people and pets back, note the exact gap, and text us a photo. Do not spray them and do not plug the hole while any bee could be inside. Once the area is confirmed empty, the opening can be closed properly.

What those few bees are actually doing

When a honey bee colony outgrows its nest, the old queen and roughly half the workers leave together. Within about twenty minutes the cloud settles into a cluster, often on a branch, a fence or the underside of a railing, and that cluster becomes a waiting room. From it, several hundred scouts fly out to look for a dark, dry hollow. Only a dozen or so of them tend to find something worth reporting.

So the bees at your eave are not residents. They are inspectors working for a swarm that may be hanging in a neighbor's tree or two streets away. Nothing has been built inside your house at this stage: no comb, no honey, no brood. That is the whole reason the first day or two matters so much. Turning the bees away costs least, and works best, while it is still only inspectors at the door.

Scouts or foragers: reading the traffic

Foragers work flowers. Around a house you see them on the hedge, the flowering tree or the birdbath, and they leave in a straight line toward their own nest. Many carry little pellets of pollen on their back legs. They have no interest in your siding.

Scouts behave differently, and the pattern is easy to spot once you know it:

  • They hover in front of one specific crack, vent or hole, facing it, drifting side to side.
  • They land, walk inside, come back out and repeat.
  • Their legs are bare, with no pollen loads.
  • The count starts at one or two and climbs over hours.

An established colony looks different again: a steady stream of bees going in and out all day, pollen coming home, and often a faint hum through the wall. If that is what you see, the scouts already won the argument, and the job becomes a wall removal or soffit removal rather than prevention.

What makes a gap worth reporting back

Scouts are fussy for good reason, since the swarm has to survive in whatever they pick. Studies of wild nests give a minimum of about 15 litres of space, with something nearer 40 litres preferred, behind a small entrance of roughly two square inches. In one published field study, a single scout spent 62 minutes inspecting one nest box.

A house offers plenty of hollows that fit. Gaps where a soffit board has shrunk away from the fascia, the open cores of concrete block, the space between wall studs, a loose roof tile over a void, or the inside of a meter or valve box all qualify. UF/IFAS extension specialists note that African honey bees will accept a much smaller cavity than European bees, which widens the list of candidate spots around an ordinary Miami-Dade home, from cement blocks and eaves to barbecue grills and holes in the ground.

How the decision reaches your wall

Back on the cluster, each scout that liked a site performs a waggle dance on the surface of the swarm. The livelier the dance, the more recruits she sends. Those recruits visit the site, judge it for themselves and may dance for it in turn, so a good cavity snowballs while weaker ones fade.

Researchers found that when roughly 10 to 15 or more scouts gather at one site, the scouts begin a vibrating signal called worker piping, which warms the swarm's flight muscles. The whole swarm takes off about an hour after that and flies to the chosen hollow. Swarms rarely hang at a waiting stop for more than three days.

In practical terms: one or two bees at a crack is early. Dozens crowding the same opening, landing, bumping into each other and running across the surface, means a decision may be close. That is the moment to get the gap looked at, not next weekend.

The first day: what helps

Start by watching from a distance, ideally from inside a window. Note the exact opening the bees favor, because once they leave it can be hard to find again. A photo or a short video of the traffic tells a removal tech a great deal, so text us one and describe roughly where on the building it is.

Next, look around the yard and nearby trees for a hanging cluster. If you find one, it is the swarm itself, and our swarm pickup crew can collect it before it moves in anywhere. Keep children and pets away from both the cluster and the gap, and hold off on mowing, hedge trimming or pressure washing near the spot, since vibration and noise draw attention from the bees.

Africanized honey bees are present in Florida, so treat any wild group as potentially defensive even when it looks calm. If anyone has been stung or bees are pouring out of the wall, call our emergency line right away; phones are answered 24/7.

Your first 48 hours with scout bees

  1. Watch from indoors and mark the exact opening the bees keep entering.
  2. Take a photo or short video of the traffic and text it to us.
  3. Check nearby trees, fences and railings for a hanging swarm cluster.
  4. Keep kids, pets and guests clear of the gap and any cluster.
  5. Pause mowing, trimming and pressure washing near the spot.
  6. Leave the opening unplugged and unsprayed until someone confirms it is empty.
  7. Once it is empty, close it with fine hardware cloth or caulk.

What not to do while they are deciding

Spraying the scouts feels like action, but it does little. The swarm has hundreds more out searching, and a few dead scouts at the entrance do not cancel the vote. Worse, if bees have already moved in, poison leaves dead comb and honey inside the wall that can leak, ferment, smell and attract pests. Extension guidance is blunt that killing a colony and sealing the remains in place is never a good idea.

Do not stuff the opening with foam, rags or tape while any bee might be inside. Bees shut into a cavity look for another way out, and the nearest exit can be a gap behind an outlet or a ceiling vent indoors. A hose blast only scatters scouts briefly, and it angers a settled colony.

Skip the ladder too, especially near the main electrical service. Extension specialists specifically warn against sealing around the power lines that enter a home. Leave high work and anything near wiring to a crew with the right gear.

If the swarm moves in anyway

Sometimes the swarm arrives before anyone can act. It looks dramatic: a swirling cloud of bees around the house that funnels into the chosen gap. Once inside, workers start building comb almost immediately, and within days the queen is laying. At that point you have a colony, and it needs to come out alive.

Our crews use thermal imaging to find the warm cluster behind block or drywall, open only what has to be opened, remove the bees and comb, and hand the colony to a beekeeper. Nothing is exterminated. Any block, board or tile that had to come off is put back and painted. That rebuild stays in house: licensed contractors, roofers and painters of our own. Should a sealed opening attract a new colony later, the warranty covers it and a crew comes back to deal with it.

Bees you can reach from the ground generally land in the $300 to $400 range. Comb tucked high under a roof or spread through a wall, with rebuilding afterward, can reach the thousands. Our pricing breakdown walks through each factor, and quoting your house is free.

Closing the door once it is empty

Scouts only matter because there is a hole to inspect. Once you are sure no bees are inside, close it. UF/IFAS advice is to close wall, chimney, electrical and plumbing gaps wider than about 30 millimetres using small-mesh hardware cloth or caulking. Number 8 hardware cloth, with eighth-inch mesh, works well over soffit vents, and foam or caulk suits the spaces around pipes and conduits.

Walk the house with fresh eyes after any big blow; our note on storm-damaged homes covers loose soffits and lifted tiles. Owners who are away for months should read bees at a seasonal home, since an empty house gives scouts days of quiet to work. If a spot has hosted bees before, the reasons are in why bees come back. Renters can check who handles bees in a rental before calling anyone.

Questions people ask us

Will scout bees sting me if I walk past the spot?

Scouts are focused on judging a cavity and have no nest to defend yet, so they rarely bother people who pass by calmly. That changes once a colony is living inside. Because Africanized bees are present in Florida, keep your distance anyway and never swat at the bees or block the opening with your hand.

The bees were gone by evening. Is it over?

Not necessarily. Scouting is daylight work, and scouts spend the night back on the swarm cluster. If the site is still in the running, they can be back the next morning. Watch the same gap the following day before deciding it lost the vote, and leave it open until you know.

Is it worth calling if nothing has moved in yet?

Yes. This is the stage where the fix is smallest. A tech can confirm whether the gap is empty, look for a nearby swarm and close the opening properly. There is no charge for the quote, and someone picks up at any hour; we stand behind a 24-hour response guarantee.

Why would scouts pick my house out of the whole street?

Scouts are hunting for a particular kind of space: dark, dry, roomy and behind a small entrance. A shrunken soffit board, an open block core or a cracked meter box can match that better than any tree nearby. Closing those gaps is what takes your house off the list.
