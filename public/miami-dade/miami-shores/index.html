Miami Shores Bee Hives in Shoreland-Era Stucco and Tile

The village was laid out in the 1920s as 'America's Mediterranean,' and its textured stucco, arched openings and tile roofs give a colony plenty of places to hide. Here is how bees get in, and how they come out alive.

Quick answer

Bee removal in Miami Shores starts with locating the colony, then taking it out alive. A heat scan pinpoints the comb behind stucco or beneath tile; bees and comb are lifted out and passed to a beekeeper, and our own licensed contractors close the opening. Expect roughly $300 to $400 for something reachable at ground level, and up into the thousands when a wall or roof has to be opened and rebuilt. Quotes cost nothing, and a person picks up the phone at any hour, day or night.

If anyone is being stung

Anyone being stung should go inside right away and close the door behind them; then call, because stinging calls jump the queue. Keep kids and animals well back from where the bees are flying, and skip the hose and the bug spray, which only stir up the colony. Trouble breathing or a swelling face means emergency medical help comes first.

In 1924 the Shoreland Company set out to build 'America's Mediterranean' on the bay north of Miami, and the hurricane of September 1926 stopped the plan before it was finished. What did get built still sets the look of the village: 118 Mediterranean-inspired houses from the Shoreland years, 25 of them now on the National Register of Historic Places, along wide streets lined with old trees. Miami Shores became a village on January 2, 1932, and it is still mostly single-family homes, with Barry University's main campus and a 1939 golf course inside its limits.

For honey bees, that mix is close to ideal. A house that has settled for nearly a century can have a hairline gap where stucco meets a window arch or where a run of tile has lifted, and a colony needs an entrance only about the width of a pencil. The canopy gives a swarm somewhere to hang while its scouts inspect those gaps. The likely hiding places here are walls, roofs, eaves and trees, and the right approach depends on which one you have.

Gaps a Shores colony is likely to use

Barrel-tile roofs

Clay tile is set over the roof deck with a dry, dark pocket under each curve. A cracked or shifted piece near the eave turns that pocket into a doorway. Bees streaming in and out under one tile is the sign to look for, and tile roof hives come out by lifting, cleaning and resetting tile.

Stucco walls and arches

Textured stucco over block or frame can hide a hollow cavity between studs or inside block cells. Watch the seams around arched windows, hose bibs and old pipe or wire holes. A colony there needs the wall opened, as our page on bees inside block and frame walls explains.

Eaves and decorative trim

Soffits, rafter tails and ornamental brackets have joints that open as wood dries and paint ages. A colony in a soffit can stretch along the eave for several feet before anyone notices. Soffit and eave hives are reached from below and the panel is replaced.

Old shade trees

Big trunks along the village's tree-lined streets develop rot pockets and hollow limbs over the years. A swarm resting on a branch may move on, but bees flying into a knothole have already built comb. Tree colonies can often come out without felling the tree.

Meter and valve boxes

Water meter boxes at the curb and sprinkler valve boxes in the lawn are dark, dry and close to the ground. Lift a lid without looking and you can be face to face with the whole colony. Leave it closed and read about meter and irrigation box removal.

Bayfront yards

On lots that run down to Biscayne Bay, bees can take shelter in dock boxes, covered boats, gaps along a seawall cap and the cabinet around pool equipment. The docks, seawalls and boats page covers how the work changes next to the water.

Why an older stucco house suits a colony

A honey bee colony wants a dry, dark cavity of roughly ten gallons with one narrow entrance it can guard. An older house can offer that in several places at once: the space between studs behind interior plaster or drywall, the cells of hollow block, the void above a ceiling where a gable vent has lost its screen. Over the decades stucco cracks at corners and where additions meet the original walls, and the caulk around window frames shrinks. None of these gaps looks like much from the sidewalk.

Once inside, bees build wax comb and fill it with honey and brood, and within a season that can add up to many pounds of material in the wall. Sealing the entrance with the bees still inside does not end the problem. Trapped bees hunt for another exit, sometimes into the house, and abandoned comb softens in the heat, leaks honey and draws pests. The colony and the comb both have to come out, which is what our comb and honey cleanup step is for.

Opening a historic wall and putting it back

Of the 118 Mediterranean-inspired houses the Shoreland Company built, 25 are on the National Register. Mediterranean Revival design typically means stuccoed walls, red tile roofs, arched windows and wrought iron balconies with window grilles, and finishes like these are hard to match if the patch is treated as an afterthought.

Before anything is cut, a thermal camera maps the warm mass of bees and brood, so the opening is only as large as the comb requires and sits where it can be blended in. The comb comes out by hand, the bees go into a ventilated box for a beekeeper, and then the wall is closed, the tile reset and the texture matched by roofers, painters and licensed contractors who work for us directly. If your house carries a historic designation, ask the village whether exterior repairs need its sign-off before work starts. Ask, and you will get photographs of every stage plus an itemized invoice to keep with the house records. Read more on repairs after removal and what a thermal scan reveals.

Swarms in the canopy

A cluster of bees the size of a football hanging from a limb over your driveway is a swarm: part of an established colony with its queen, resting while scouts check possible homes. Swarming bees are full of honey and tend to be calm, and many clusters leave within a day or two. The question is where they go next. On a street of older houses the scouts may choose your soffit or a wall cavity over a hollow tree.

Your part is simple: clear the area under the branch, leave the cluster unsprayed, and phone us. Collecting a cluster before it moves in is simpler and costs less than cutting comb out of a wall later, as the swarm pickup page describes. A handful of bees poking at one crack may be scouts, covered in our guide to scout bees. And if bees return to a spot after a removal, this guide explains the scent that draws them and why the seal matters.

Cost, timing and paperwork

Price follows access. When a crew member standing on the lawn can reach the bees, say a cluster in a hedge or a colony in a curbside meter box, the visit is generally priced about $300 to $400. A hive built into a wall cavity or under a roof is a different job: an opening, hand removal of the comb, then a rebuild, and the biggest of those climb into the thousands. After dark and on weekends, an emergency visit is billed at a higher rate than the same work on a weekday. You pay nothing for the quote, and our cost breakdown shows which parts of a job drive the total.

There is no hour when the phone goes to a machine, and each request falls under our 24-hour response guarantee. Shops on the boulevard or Northeast 2nd Avenue, and any HOA or condo board, can have a COI, meaning proof of our insurance, sent over before the crew pulls up. How a visit to a Shores home runs

  1. Photo first, then a call

    Send a picture of the entry point and tell us whether it is a wall, roof, tree or box. If anyone has been stung, say so first; that moves you to the front.

  2. A walk around with the camera

    The crew parks on your street, walks the outside of the house with a thermal camera and marks where the comb sits. You hear the price before any tile is lifted or stucco cut.

  3. Bees out, comb out

    A low-suction bee vacuum moves the colony into a screened box, and the comb is cut free for a beekeeper. Every scrap of honey and wax leaves with the crew, because leftovers can sour in the wall and pull in another swarm.

  4. Sealed, rebuilt, warrantied

    Our contractors close the entry, rebuild the opening and match the finish. The work is warrantied, so bees back in a spot we closed means we drive back out.

Questions people ask us

My house is one of the 1920s Mediterranean ones. Will removal ruin the original stucco?

Taking a colony out of a wall means making an opening, but it can be small and carefully placed. The thermal scan shows where the comb actually is, so nobody is guessing with a saw. Afterward our own contractors and painters rebuild the section, blending stucco texture and paint color into the wall around it. Ask for photos of each stage if you keep records on the house.

Bees are going in and out under one roof tile. Can I just seal it?

Please don't. Bees shut in under the tile search for another way out, and some may end up in the attic or the house. The comb left behind softens in summer heat and the honey can leak and stain. The colony comes out first, then the tile is reset and the gap closed. See tile roof work for the steps.

Will the bees be killed?

No. The colony leaves your property alive and goes to a beekeeper, who moves it to a new home; live removal is all we do. Honey bees are pollinators, and a colony from your yard can be kept somewhere it bothers nobody. Read about relocation to beekeepers.

There's a swarm in the tree out front. Do I have to do anything?

Many swarms leave on their own once the scouts agree on a new home, often a day or two later. Keep people and pets at a distance and don't spray it. If it is still there the next day, hangs over a doorway or sidewalk, or the bees start going into a hole, call so it can be collected before it settles into a building.

I run a shop on Northeast 2nd Avenue. Can you work around my hours?

Yes. Tell us your hours and whether the landlord wants a COI on file; it can be sent before the visit. Early or after-hours work can be arranged. Keep in mind that a night or weekend emergency is billed above a regular weekday call. More on commercial removals.
