Bees in Bay Harbor Islands walls and roofs, taken out alive

Bay Harbor Islands fits garden apartments, flat roofs and single-family yards onto two small islands. When honey bees settle into any of them, they leave in a beekeeper's box, still alive.

Quick answer

Honey bees inside a Bay Harbor Islands wall, roof edge or breeze-block screen can be taken out alive, and that is the only way this crew works. A technician pins down the comb, frequently by thermal scan, lifts out bees and honeycomb by hand, and sends the colony to a beekeeper. Ground-level jobs tend to cost about $300 to $400; colonies deep in a structure that needs repair cost more, sometimes into the thousands. Quotes cost nothing, a person answers the phone at any hour, and a 24-hour response guarantee backs every call.

If anyone is being stung

If a neighbor is getting stung in a shared corridor or by the pool, move the person inside, close the door behind you, and then call. People come before the colony every time. Keep neighbors off that stair or corridor, switch off any blower or pressure washer near the bees, and do not spray them, since that tends to turn a few defenders into a cloud.

Around Bay Harbor Islands

  • IncorporatedApril 28, 1947
  • LandTwo filled islands, West and East, about 0.4 square miles of land
  • Mainland linkShepard Broad Causeway, a toll drawbridge run by the town, opened 1951
  • HousingSingle-family homes on West Island; apartments and the business district on East Island
  • Residents5,922 at the 2020 census

Bay Harbor Islands started in 1947 as two mangrove islands that Shepard Broad filled, paying partly with down payments on lot sales, and the town still splits the same way. The West Island is single-family homes. The East Island holds the business district, the K-8 school and the multi-family buildings, and it once held one of the largest concentrated collections of mid-century Miami Modern design, MiMo for short.

That split decides what a bee problem looks like here. On the west side you are dealing with one owner, one roofline and a yard. On the east side the colony may sit in a decorative block wall shared by a dozen units, above an open-air walkway everyone uses, or inside a parapet on a flat roof that only the building manager can reach. Honey bees do not care which. A scout hunting for a home wants a dry cavity with a small entrance, and mid-century concrete offers a lot of them.

Block cores, parapets and other cavities bees pick here

Breeze-block screens

Decorative screen block shades MiMo stairwells and balconies, and where a screen ties into hollow-core block or a stucco column, bees can slip into the voids behind it. Comb there stays hidden from both faces, so the fix is opening the right blocks, not the whole screen.

Flat-roof parapets

MiMo apartment roofs are typically flat, often edged by low parapet walls and flashing. Where that flashing has lifted, bees gain a sheltered void just above the top-floor ceiling, and the first clue may be bees on the roof deck or a stain indoors.

Walkway ceilings

Open-air walkways linking the units carry a ceiling cavity for lights and conduit. A colony up there puts its flight path right over the doors, so residents meet bees on the way to their own apartments. That calls for a clear plan for the corridor.

West Island rooflines and yards

Single-family houses bring the familiar spots: soffits and eaves, gaps along the roof edge, hollow trees and palm boots if your yard has them. Detached sheds and garage walls count too, since they sit closed and quiet for days.

Docks and boat lifts

If your lot faces the bay or Indian Creek, check dock boxes, lift housings and folded covers. Waterfront gear can sit untouched for weeks, which hands a passing swarm a ready-made cavity a few steps from the water.

Notes for each part of town

  • West Island

    This is the single-family side of town, so a colony is likely to be in a house wall, an eave or a tree, and you can sign off on the work yourself. Mention gates, dogs and whether a neighbor shares the hedge or wall line where the bees are flying.

  • East Island

    Apartments, condominiums, the business district and the K-8 school share this island. Bees in a common wall or roof are the association's decision, so bring the manager in early. A COI goes to the board before we arrive, and we can work around resident schedules.

  • Kane Concourse and 96th Street

    State Road 922 crosses the islands along Kane Concourse and leaves by 96th Street toward State Road A1A. Shops line this stretch, so for a storefront job we ask where a truck can stand and when work in front of the door is least disruptive.

  • Along Indian Creek

    Indian Creek separates the East Island from Bal Harbour and Surfside. Waterfront lots on that edge may have docks, decks and pool areas facing the creek, and a colony on one bank is a short flight from gardens on the other.

  • The causeway end

    The Shepard Broad Causeway brings you in from North Miami across the bay. The town operates it, it carries a toll and it has a drawbridge, and a bridge opening can hold traffic for a while, so arrival plans include some slack.

Mid-century concrete and the bees that find it

Builders on the East Island leaned on the MiMo toolkit: flat roofs, decorative breeze block, open-air walkways linking the units and symmetrical staircases. Coral Sea Towers, a 1956 design by Carlos B. Schoeppl, is one listed example in town. Concrete and stucco will not rot, but they hide comb well, and comb packed into hollow block or a parapet void cannot be pulled out through the small hole the bees use.

So the thermal camera comes out before anyone cuts. A live brood nest runs warmer than the wall around it, and the image narrows the opening to the blocks or panel that actually hold comb. After the live removal, our own licensed contractors and painters patch and match the surface, and comb and honey cleanup takes out the leftover wax that would otherwise draw the next swarm. The field guide on what a thermal scan shows explains the camera in more detail.

Condo boards and the milestone calendar

Florida law requires milestone structural inspections for condominium and co-op buildings of three or more habitable stories, due by the end of the year a building turns 30, and local officials may move that to 25 years where salt water is close. In a town incorporated in 1947, a building from its early decades is well past both marks.

Inspection and concrete repair work opens soffits, balcony edges and walls, and an opened cavity is exactly where a hidden colony comes to light. If bees turn up mid-project, pause the crew at that spot and call. The colony comes out alive, the association gets photos and an itemized invoice on request, and we coordinate with your contractor so the area is ready for their repair. Managers running several buildings can read the notes for HOAs and property managers and for condo buildings.

Swarms on an island ringed by water

The town says Bay Harbor Islands lies entirely in a floodplain, wedged between the open bay and Indian Creek. Heavy weather can strip a soffit or crack a parapet and leave comb exposed, so after a blow it pays to look around; the guide on colonies exposed after a storm lists what to inspect.

In spring a crowded colony splits, and the swarm hangs on a railing, hedge or palm while its scouts search for a new cavity. A cluster like that is usually calm and may leave on its own, but it can also pick your parapet or meter box. Read about scout bees checking out the house and ask for a swarm pickup before the bees start building comb.

Questions people ask us

Who pays when bees are in a shared wall of an East Island condo?

Walls, roofs and walkways are normally common elements, so the association usually authorizes and pays, but your declaration has the final word. A colony entirely inside your own unit can be a different story. Either way the quote is free, the board can have a COI, and renters can read who handles bees in a rental.

Can you get bees out of breeze block without tearing down the screen wall?

Usually only part of it has to open. The thermal scan shows which cores hold comb, and only those blocks or the face behind them get opened. Our own crew then replaces or patches the units and paints to match. Original decorative block can be hard to find, so we talk through options with you or the board before cutting.

Will you kill the bees if the building wants them gone fast?

No. Killing them is off the table; the bees leave alive and end up with a local keeper. Poisoning bees inside a wall also leaves dead bees and softening comb behind, which can draw pests and new swarms. Live removal followed by cleanup is what keeps the spot clear.

Do night or weekend calls cost more here?

Yes. Emergency visits at night or on weekends are priced above a weekday appointment. Ground-level jobs generally fall around $300 to $400, while work that opens a roof or wall and needs repairs can climb into the thousands. See the cost page for the factors behind a quote.
