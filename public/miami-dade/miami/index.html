City of Miami, neighborhood by neighborhood

Bee removal in Miami, from frame cottages on the river to glass towers on the bay

The city grew in layers: frame houses along the river, 1920s boom streets, MiMo hotels on Biscayne Boulevard, then the skyline. Bees use every layer, and each one comes apart differently when the comb has to come out.

Quick answer

Inside the City of Miami, the age of the building decides how a colony comes out: wood siding on the early frame cottages by the river, barrel tile and parapets on 1920s boom houses, hollow block on postwar apartments, and management sign-off in the Brickell towers. Whatever yours is, leave the entry crack unplugged and unsprayed, keep kids and pets back, and send a photo. A beekeeper takes the live colony, the wax is removed down to the last sheet, our own crews close the opening, ground-level work runs about $300 to $400, and the quote is free.

If anyone is being stung

Being stung, or watching bees pour out of a wall? Head for the nearest car or building with a shirt or towel held across your face, and close the door behind you. Skip the pool and the hose. Anyone wheezing or swelling around the face needs 911 first; after that, ring us at any hour and the sting call jumps the queue.

The City of Miami is a smaller place than the name on most envelopes suggests. It is the municipality incorporated in 1896, about 36 square miles running from the Upper East Side down through Brickell and Coconut Grove, and out west along Flagler Street to Flagami. Inside that line sit the city's oldest surviving frame buildings, whole streets of 1920s Mediterranean Revival and Mission homes, block apartment buildings from the postwar years, and the third-largest skyline in the country.

That range matters to a bee colony and to whoever has to take it out. A swarm doesn't care whether it lands in an early frame cottage or on the 30th floor; it wants a dry, dark cavity with a small entrance, and the city offers thousands of them. What changes from block to block is how the comb is reached: through pine siding, under barrel tile, behind stucco on hollow block, or through a balcony planter that a condo board has to sign off on. This page walks through the city by building type and by neighborhood so you know what you are probably dealing with before anyone opens a wall.

Spots in Miami construction that bees move into

Old frame walls

The South River Drive area holds the city's oldest surviving group of vernacular frame buildings. In houses like these, a colony fills the bay between two studs behind wood siding, and a loose board or a gap at the sill is enough of a door. Removal means lifting siding carefully, not knocking holes.

Parapets and barrel tile

Mission and Mediterranean Revival homes from the boom years often pair flat roof sections behind low parapet walls with barrel-tile accents. Bees slip in at scuppers, under loose tiles and where the parapet cap has cracked, then build comb down into the roof framing.

Hollow block walls

Postwar apartments, duplexes and many mid-century commercial buildings are commonly concrete block under stucco. The hollow cores run up and along the wall, so a colony that enters at a pipe penetration or a hose bib can sit two or three feet from where you see the bees.

Balconies and AC closets

High-rise towers in Brickell, Downtown and Edgewater give bees planter boxes, hollow railings, unused AC closets and the voids around rooftop equipment. Height doesn't stop a swarm, and the work has to fit around building rules, elevators and neighbors.

Swale boxes and old trees

The concrete lids over water meters and sprinkler valves along the swale hide a dark, dry chamber that colonies take readily, and the same goes for cavities in big yard trees, from dooryard mangoes to the banyans along Coral Way. A box lid with bees under it is something to step away from, not lift.

River boatyards and yards

The Miami River is still a working river, with shipyards and marine businesses along its banks in Allapattah and elsewhere. Stacked materials, idle equipment and dry-stored hulls make sheltered cavities that nobody checks for weeks.

Neighborhood notes inside the city line

  • Brickell

    Once platted for mansions on Brickell Avenue, now Miami's densest neighborhood and its financial district, with the towers of Brickell Key just offshore. Expect concierge check-in, loading-dock times and a management office that wants insurance paperwork before anyone goes up.

  • Downtown

    Greater Downtown sits east of I-95 on both sides of the river, and three historic districts in and around it keep buildings from the 1890s through the mid-20th century. Older masonry here has ledges, cornices and vents that colonies like; street-level access and parking shape how the visit is planned.

  • Little Havana

    Between SW 3rd and SW 37th Avenues, with Calle Ocho through the middle. The Riverview Historic District mixes bungalows, Mission, Mediterranean Revival and Miami Modern homes, and the South River Drive district near the river keeps the city's oldest group of frame buildings, so wood walls turn up.

  • Wynwood

    A garment district from the 1920s and later a warehouse area, now painted walls, galleries and new mid-rise buildings. Old warehouse roofs, parapets and roll-up door housings give bees room, and businesses usually want the work timed around opening hours.

  • Edgewater

    Bayfront high-rises along the water, with early 20th-century houses still standing on the inland streets. One block can mean a balcony job with a condo board; the next can mean a stucco cottage wall with a side yard and a gate.

  • Upper East Side, MiMo and Morningside

    The MiMo district lines Biscayne Boulevard from about 50th to 77th Street with 1950s and 1960s Miami Modern hotels. East of it, the Morningside Historic District was built mostly between 1922 and 1941, with Mediterranean, Art Deco and vernacular houses on tree-lined streets near the bay.

  • Buena Vista

    Between Little Haiti and the Design District. The Buena Vista East Historic District is mainly 1920s single-family homes in Mediterranean Revival, Mission, Craftsman and Art Deco styles, so tile, parapets and old wood eaves all show up here.

  • Little Haiti and Lemon City

    Called Lemon City for well over a century, and also known historically as Little River and Edison Center, the area was officially designated Little Haiti by the city in 2016. Where a lot has a shed, a fence line or a mature tree, an outdoor colony is as likely as one in a wall.

  • Allapattah and Overtown

    Allapattah, annexed in 1925, mixes houses with produce markets, garment shops and shipyards along the river. Overtown, the historic center of Black Miami's commerce, sits beside I-95 and SR 836. Both have older buildings next to warehouses and open lots.

  • Shenandoah, Silver Bluff and Flagami

    Shenandoah, a 1919 subdivision, has revival-style homes between Calle Ocho and Coral Way. Silver Bluff, annexed in 1926, adds 1920s bungalows and Mission Revival houses along Coral Way. Out west, Flagami is small single-family homes, duplexes and apartments by the Tamiami Canal.

A Miami address is not always the City of Miami

Plenty of people who write Miami on their mail live outside the city. Miami-Dade has 34 incorporated municipalities, and roughly 44 percent of the county's residents live in unincorporated areas served directly by the county. A postal city follows mail routes, not municipal lines, so places such as Westchester or Kendall can share a Miami label with Brickell.

The quickest way to tell is your property folio number from the county property appraiser. The first two digits are the municipal code: 01 means the City of Miami, and 30 means unincorporated Miami-Dade. It matters less for bees than for paperwork, since a repair that needs a permit goes through whichever government your property falls under.

For removal it makes no difference. We work the whole county, and the Miami-Dade service area page lists every place by region. If your street sits in the Grove, the Coconut Grove page covers its canopy and older homes; if you are just over the line in the Gables, see Coral Gables.

Four building eras, four different jobs

Buildings in the city fall into roughly four kinds of structure, and the method follows the material.

  • Wood frame from the early 1900s: siding comes off board by board, the comb is cut from between the studs, and the boards go back on or get replaced to match.
  • Boom-era stucco with tile and parapets: the roofer lifts and resets tile, and flat sections are opened from above. See tile and flat roof hives.
  • Concrete block under stucco: thermal imaging traces the warm cluster through the cores before a small opening is cut, then the block is patched and painted. The method for each wall type is laid out on our wall removal page.
  • Towers: the work is scheduled with management and done from a balcony, a roof or a mechanical room, often with less cutting than a house but more paperwork.

Asking which era your building comes from is the first useful step, because it tells you whether the fix is a carpenter's job, a roofer's job or a mason's job. Carpentry, roofing and block patching all come from our own licensed crews, so one company answers for the finished wall.

Condo towers, boards and managers

A colony on the 20th floor still needs to be handled live, but the steps around it are different. Building management usually wants a COI naming the association before work starts, an elevator reserved, and a window of time that keeps residents off a shared balcony or out of a stairwell.

We send COIs for condos, HOAs and businesses on request, and we can work from the unit, the roof or a common area depending on where the cavity is. If the bees are in a planter or a railing, the fix is often small; if they have gone into a wall between units, thermal imaging shows which side to open so only one owner loses a section of drywall.

Property managers can read more on condo and high-rise removals and HOA and commercial work. Owners who split time between Miami and somewhere else will want bees at a seasonal home, since a tower unit can sit empty for months while a colony grows in an AC closet.

Why nobody should poke at a Miami colony

African honey bees and their hybrids are present in Florida, and University of Florida entomologists describe them as defending a wider area around the nest, reacting to less disturbance, and sending far more bees after an intruder than European bees do. From the outside you can't tell which kind you have.

That is why a hose, a can of spray or a shovel at a meter box is a bad idea anywhere in the city, and especially on tight lots where the neighbor's kids, a dog run or a bus stop is a few feet away. Spray also kills bees without removing the comb, and abandoned comb melts, ferments and draws the next swarm to the same spot.

If bees are already in the air around you, move away fast and get inside a building or a car. Water won't hide you either, because bees linger over a swimming pool or the bay until you come up. Then call. Phones are answered day and night, someone being stung goes to the front of the line, and our 24-hour response guarantee holds whether you ring at noon or at 3 a.m.

Yards, swales and old trees

Not every Miami colony is in a building. The city has big yard trees, from dooryard mangoes that bloom between December and April to the 1,200 banyans planted down the Coral Way median in 1929. Old limbs rot into hollows, and a colony in a trunk can stay put for years.

Down at the curb, the meter and sprinkler boxes are a known nest site, because they are dark, dry and seldom opened. Sheds, stacked pavers, a boat under a cover and the space under a stored grill all do the same job.

Tree colonies are usually cut out of the cavity or removed with the section of limb, depending on the tree and what the owner wants to keep; see palm and tree colonies. For a box, the comb is lifted out piece by piece and the lid gets sealed; see meter, valve and irrigation boxes. If you are only seeing a handful of bees checking a crack, read scout bees checking out the house before anything moves in.

What happens to the house afterward

Getting the bees out solves today. Comb left in a wall softens in Miami heat, honey seeps into drywall and stucco, and the smell of old wax tells the next swarm where a good home used to be. So the comb is cut out completely and the cavity is cleaned; comb and honey cleanup explains what that involves.

Closing up is done in-house as well: the carpenter on your siding, the roofer on your tile and the painter on your stucco are all our own licensed people. On a frame house that might be new siding and trim; on block, a patch and paint match; under tile, reset tile and new underlayment. The details are on the repairs after removal page.

That closure carries a workmanship warranty, which in practice means we come back out if a new colony gets past the seal. If your HOA or insurer wants a record, we can document the open wall and the finished patch in photos and break the bill down item by item. The scent that old wax leaves for future swarms is the subject of our field guide piece on bees returning to an old nest site.

What sets the price on a city job

The low end of the range, about $300 to $400, covers colonies someone can reach while standing on the ground: a swale box, a low shrub, an eave just overhead. The price climbs when the comb is deep inside a structure: siding to lift on a frame house, tile to reset on a boom-era roof, a block wall to open and patch, or a tower job with lifts, staging and building rules. Complex removals that include repairs can reach the thousands.

Timing counts too. Night and weekend visits are billed above the weekday rate, and emergencies are still taken at those hours. The quote is free, and a clear photo of the entrance can often show which of these categories you are in. The cost page goes through each factor, and if the bees turned up during a sale, bees found during a home inspection covers timing with a closing date.

What the appointment looks like in Miami

  1. Photo and address check

    Send the photo and the address. We confirm whether it is a house, a duplex or a tower unit, and whether a board, a landlord or a manager has to approve access first.

  2. Getting in

    For towers, the visit is booked through the front desk or management, with a COI sent ahead and an elevator held if needed. For houses, a gate code, a dog put away and a clear side yard are enough.

  3. Finding the comb

    Thermal imaging shows where the cluster sits inside block, frame or ceiling, so the opening is cut in the right place and kept as small as the comb allows.

  4. Out, cleaned, closed

    A gentle vacuum collects the bees for a beekeeper, every piece of comb comes out, and our own crew closes the hole, either before leaving or on a scheduled return.

Questions people ask us

My address says Miami, but am I actually in the City of Miami?

Check the folio number on your property tax bill or the county property appraiser's site. If it starts with 01, you are in the City of Miami; 30 means unincorporated Miami-Dade, and other codes belong to other municipalities. We serve all of them, so it only matters for repair permits and paperwork.

There are bees going into the wood siding of my old house. Will you have to tear it apart?

No. On frame houses the siding is lifted in the section where the thermal camera shows the colony, the wax sheets are trimmed free of the framing, and the boards are refastened or swapped for matching stock. The goal is the smallest opening that lets all the comb out.

Our condo has bees in a balcony planter on a high floor. Who arranges this?

Either the owner or management can call. Management usually needs a COI and an elevator booking, and we will send the insurance paperwork directly to the office. A planter or railing colony usually needs far less cutting than one inside a wall.

Would a can of bug spray down the water meter lid at the curb take care of it?

Please don't. Spray rarely reaches the whole colony, it can set off a defensive response on a sidewalk where people walk past, and dead comb in the box keeps attracting new swarms. Leave the lid shut and keep people away until the bees are removed live.

I rent in Little Havana and the bees are in the building. Who should make the call?

Tell your landlord in writing first, since repairs to the building are usually the owner's call, but anyone can phone us if someone is in danger. The renter and landlord guide explains how these calls usually get sorted out and who signs the quote.

Will that clump of bees on the mango out back in Edgewater move on by itself?

A ball of bees on a branch is usually a traveling swarm parked for a rest, waiting on its scouts to pick a new cavity, and it may be gone in a day or two. If it is near a door or a walkway, or if it starts building comb on the branch, call and it can be collected live; see swarm pickup.

We had a storm and now there is comb showing in a cracked wall. What now?

Exposed comb draws robbing bees and the colony can turn defensive, so keep people back from that side of the house. Send a photo and we will plan removal and the repair together. The after a storm guide covers what to check first.
