Bee removal in Doral, from loading docks to townhouse eaves

Doral mixes business parks and family neighborhoods inside fifteen square miles. Honey bees use both, and the fix looks different in a distribution center than in a two-story townhouse.

Quick answer

If honey bees are flying in and out of one gap on a Doral building, a colony has probably moved in behind it. Keep people and pets back, skip the spray, and text us one clear shot of where they enter. A crew takes the bees out alive, cuts out the comb so it cannot rot or draw a new swarm, and seals the opening. Bees go to a beekeeper. Ground-level work usually runs about $300 to $400; jobs that need wall or roof repairs cost more.

If anyone is being stung

Someone being stung needs distance first: walk them inside or into a vehicle and shut the doors. Dial 911 at once if their breath gets short, their mouth or eyelids swell, or the stings keep adding up. Then phone us. Calls are picked up around the clock and anyone being stung is handled before routine jobs.

Around Doral

In the late 1950s the Kaskels bought 2,400 acres of swampland here for a golf club, and the city still runs out to the Everglades on its western side. Honey bee colonies multiply by swarming, most heavily in spring, and a scout looking for a dry, shaded cavity finds plenty of them in Doral: warehouse parapets, the hollow ends of townhouse rows, stucco walls on newer single-family streets.

The city's land use plan has a full industrial category for warehouses, showrooms and distribution centers, and 14 of the county's business parks sit inside city limits, so plenty of bee problems here turn up on commercial property. Those calls come with tenants, insurance paperwork and business hours to work around. Residential calls come with neighbors who share a wall. This page covers both, so you can tell what you are looking at and what the visit will involve.

Where colonies settle on Doral buildings

Warehouse parapets

The low wall around a flat warehouse roof often hides a gap where flashing has pulled loose. Bees enter from above, out of sight from the parking lot, and the first sign is usually a line of them crossing the dock apron.

Dock and signage boxes

Sign cabinets, light housings and hollow canopy framing over loading docks are enclosed and rarely opened. A colony can fill one with comb before anyone on the floor notices more than a few bees near the door.

Townhouse soffits

Attached townhomes share long eave lines, and a vent screen that fails on one unit gives bees a path into the soffit that can run past the next owner's ceiling. Opening it may involve two households.

Stucco block walls

On detached homes, bees slip through weep gaps, pipe penetrations and cracked stucco into the hollow cells of concrete block. Thermal imaging shows how far the comb reaches before anything gets cut.

Golf course trees

Mature trees and palms along fairways and backyard edges have cavities and dense frond skirts. Swarms rest on branches here, and some stay long enough to start building comb in the open.

Bees at a dock door or an eave line?

One clear picture of the hole they use is often enough to judge whether it is a swarm, a young colony or an established one.

Warehouses, showrooms and leased space

In a warehouse or showroom, the person who notices the bees is rarely the person who can approve the work. Send a photo to whoever manages the building, and ask them to call us or send the quote form. Building owners usually want insurance on file first. The crew is licensed and insured, and the COI can list whichever company manages the property.

Workers should stay away from the opening and avoid running forklifts or pressure washers right beside it, because vibration and noise set guard bees off. Do not tape over the hole. Bees sealed inside look for another way out, and that can be an outlet or light fixture on the office side of the wall. A thermal camera lets the crew map the colony first, and repairs to parapets, flashing or panels are handled by our own licensed roofers and contractors. For larger sites, see work for commercial and property managers and flat roof hives.

When the bees share a wall with your neighbor

Townhouse colonies are trickier than detached ones because the comb may cross into the unit next door. Before we open anything, it helps to know who owns the exterior and whether the association handles soffits and stucco. Let the neighbor know about the visit so nobody opens a window on the wrong side.

The crew vacuums the bees into a ventilated box for a beekeeper, cuts out the full comb along with the stored honey, and closes the gap so new scouts are not drawn to the scent. Our painters match the patch to your trim. Photos and an itemized invoice are available, which helps when an HOA needs proof of the repair. Our workmanship carries a warranty: should a new colony move into the patched section, the crew returns to deal with it. More detail is on soffit and eave hives and bees in block walls.

Swarms on a branch versus a colony in a wall

A swarm is a cluster of bees hanging from a branch, a fence or a car mirror. It has built nothing yet, and it tends to lift off once its scouts agree on a new home, often by the next morning or the one after. Give it space and keep children away. When one settles over an entry, a patio or the path to the car, it can be boxed up live and driven to a beekeeper.

A colony is different. Bees going steadily in and out of one hole, carrying pollen, means comb and brood are already inside. That will not leave on its own, and spraying it only leaves dead bees and melting honey in the wall. Three or four bees circling a Doral eave for an afternoon are often scouts on a house hunt; the dangling ball of bees belongs under swarm pickup.

How a Doral visit runs

  1. Access and paperwork

    For a business, we confirm the site contact, dock access and any COI the owner needs on file. For an HOA community, we ask about gate entry and who owns the exterior.

  2. Finding the colony

    The crew watches the entrance, then uses a thermal camera on the wall, parapet or soffit to see where the warm cluster sits and how big it is.

  3. Removal and sealing

    Bees are vacuumed out alive and the comb is cut away. Licensed contractors close the cavity and patch stucco, flashing or trim so the spot is not reused.

Questions people ask us

Who pays for bees inside a leased warehouse in Doral?

That depends on your lease. Some leases make roof and exterior repairs the landlord's job, while the tenant handles the interior. Send the lease section and photos to the owner. Either party can get the quote, and whoever pays can have a COI and an itemized invoice.

Can removal happen before our warehouse opens for the day?

Yes. A commercial removal can be scheduled for early morning or after closing so staff and customers are not near the opening. After-dark and weekend emergency calls are priced higher than a weekday appointment, so a colony that is not threatening anyone is cheaper to book midweek.

Our townhouse colony seems to extend into the unit next door. Who handles that?

Talk to your neighbor and your association before the visit. The comb can cross the party wall, and we need access from whichever side gets us to it cleanly. We remove the whole colony, not just the half on your side, and repair the opening afterward.

Will my HOA accept the repair?

Our own licensed contractors and painters do the patch, and we can photograph each step. If the association wants a COI or proof of the work for its files, mention it when you book and it will be sent over.

Do the bees get killed during removal?

No. Honey bees are collected alive and taken to a beekeeper to be relocated. We do not exterminate them, and we also remove the comb so the cavity is not left full of honey that draws a new swarm.
