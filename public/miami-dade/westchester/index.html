Bee removal for Westchester's mid-century houses and the lots around them

Westchester filled in fast after a 1955 subdivision gave it a name. Seventy years of re-roofing, add-ons and patched stucco leave plenty of openings for a honey bee colony to move into.

Quick answer

Most Westchester houses went up after the 1955 subdivision that named the area, so each has been through several re-roofs, added rooms and stucco patches. That history decides the job: the colony usually sits in a seam where old and new work meet, and a thermal scan finds it before any cut. Bees, wax and stored honey are all lifted out, a beekeeper takes the live colony, and the seam is closed by our own trades. Budget about $300 to $400 for a low, easy colony, up into the thousands if the old block or tile needs rebuilding, and nothing for the quote.

If anyone is being stung

If someone has been stung several times or bees are pouring out of a wall right now, get everyone inside and close windows and doors. Do not spray the nest or block the hole; that sends more bees out. A person who is wheezing, dizzy or puffing up around the lips needs an ambulance first, so dial 911. Once that's handled, phone us at any hour; a call about active stinging jumps the line.

Around Westchester

  • 2020 census56,384 people after the University Park CDP was folded in

For most of its history this land was too wet to settle. It was ruled ineligible under the 1862 Homestead Act because of seasonal rains and flooding, and only canal digging made it dry ground by the 1920s. Even then it stayed mostly empty until a subdivision called Westchester was platted in 1955. Miracle Manor, Mirador, Westchester Park, Town Park and Coral Park followed through the 1960s, and the streets between Tamiami Trail and Bird Road became the grid of single-family houses you see today.

That timing matters for bees. A house built in 1958 has been re-roofed several times, has probably gained a room or lost a carport, and wears coat after coat of paint and caulk. Each change leaves a seam. A swarm hunting for a dark, dry cavity finds those seams quickly, and once a colony is inside it will build comb until the space is full. A steady two-way stream of traffic at one crack, hour after hour, means a nest sits behind it rather than a passing visit. The page on scout bees explains how to tell the two apart.

Weak points a 1950s or 1960s house develops

Where an addition meets the original wall

Many mid-century homes have had rooms added or a carport closed in. The joint between old and new construction rarely lines up perfectly, and a gap behind trim or at the roofline can open into a hollow the size of a cooler. See bees inside a wall.

Soffits and fascia boards

Old wood soffits dry, split and get patched with whatever was handy. On a house this age, the soffit you see may be the third one fitted, and any corner where the patching stopped short is an open door into the attic edge, where a colony can fill the bays between rafters. That job is covered under soffit and eave hives.

Under re-laid roof tile

Every re-roof changes how tile, flashing and underlayment meet. A loose barrel tile near a valley or a chimney chase gives bees a sheltered pocket that stays dry in summer storms. More on tile and flat roof colonies.

Meter, valve and irrigation boxes

Ground boxes in a front swale are dark, shallow and left alone for months. A small colony in one is often the lowest-cost kind of job because there is nothing to cut open. The utility box page has details.

Mature yard trees and palms

Lots laid out in the 1950s have had decades to grow big shade trees. A hollow limb or the boot of a palm can hold a colony that you only notice when someone mows close. See palm and tree colonies.

A line of bees on the side of the house?

Stand back, photograph the crack or vent they file into, and send it over. That image is what the free quote is built on.

Notes on Westchester's subdivisions and corridors

  • The original 1955 Westchester tract

    The subdivision that named the whole area is among the oldest housing stock here. If your house still has its original walls plus a later addition, the joint between them is the first place to check when bees appear.

  • Miracle Manor and Mirador

    Both arrived in the 1960s wave. If your house has a low-pitched roof with long eaves, a colony can spread along the soffit well past the hole you can see from the yard.

  • Coral Park

    The name shows up on the local high school, an elementary school and a church on SW 16th Street. The subdivision dates to the same 1960s run of building, and school-day traffic is worth planning around when the crew parks.

  • Westchester Park and Town Park

    Two more 1960s subdivisions from the same building spree. If your plat or closing papers name one of them, your house is roughly sixty years old, which tells you to expect some past repair work hiding in the walls.

  • University Park and the FIU side

    The west half of today's Westchester was its own census area until it was merged in before the 2020 count. The university campus sits on the old Tamiami Airport, and Tamiami Park holds the rest of that land. Big open green space next to houses means foraging bees nearby.

  • The Bird Road corridor

    Along the southern edge, Bird Road runs as a six-lane, mostly commercial road. Storefront parapets, sign cabinets and rooftop units are typical places for bees on a business, and a landlord or manager will usually want proof of insurance on file first.

  • SW 16th Street and 87th Avenue

    This corner holds the mid-century modern building where Temple Or Olom was established, now known as the whale church. Curved, unusual rooflines on institutional buildings create overhangs and voids that are hard to inspect from the ground, which is where thermal imaging helps.

Why seventy-year-old houses keep attracting bees

A honey bee swarm is choosy. Scouts look for a cavity that is dark, dry, protected from wind and roughly the volume of a small trash can, with a narrow entrance they can defend. A brand-new house rarely offers that. A house from the late 1950s, after a few rounds of renovations, often does. Old stucco cracks follow the lines of block or framing below it. A bathroom fan that was removed and plastered over can leave an open chase. A soffit vent that was painted shut on the outside may still be open on the inside. None of this is a sign of neglect; it is what happens to any building over decades. The practical point is that a colony you can see in one spot may be using a void that runs much farther. Before anything is cut, a thermal camera is used to read where the warm cluster of bees and comb actually sits, so the saw goes in over the comb itself and the hole stays no bigger than the job needs. If bees keep returning to a house that has had a hive before, why bees come back explains the scent and wax residue that draws them.

Parks, campus lawns and the forage around you

Westchester and its edges are not short on green space. Just across Bird Road from the southeast corner, Tropical Park, the county park on the old racetrack grounds by the Palmetto Expressway, covers 275 acres and has four lakes and an equestrian center. On the west side, the university campus and Tamiami Park share the old airport land. Large open grounds with flowering trees and water give bee colonies forage and a place to drink, and a strong colony nearby will eventually throw off swarms. A swarm clumped on a fence or branch is a homeless colony resting while scouts search, and it tends to be gentle in that state. Most leave on their own after a short stay. When one lingers past the second morning, or you notice scouts working your eaves, get it picked up before the cluster chooses your house. The swarm pickup page covers what that involves, and our storm guide explains why fallen trees after high wind can suddenly expose a colony that has lived in a yard unnoticed.

Older owners, adult children and keeping it simple

The 2020 census put Westchester's median age at 45.3, with nearly a quarter of residents 65 or older. If your parents bought here long ago and you now handle repairs for them from across town, you can run the whole thing from your phone: snap the entrance hole, send it over, read the quote and say yes. Photos of the opened cavity and an itemized invoice are available when you ask, which helps if you are keeping a parent informed or filing paperwork. Patching the stucco, re-hanging a soffit or resetting tile falls to the same company that pulled the bees, using our own licensed trades, which means one bill and nobody else to chase. For homes that sit empty part of the year, the seasonal home guide has more.

Schools, churches and the businesses on Bird Road

Westchester has a heavy mix of institutional buildings: a college seminary whose construction began in the late 1950s, Catholic high schools, churches and a regional library on Coral Way. Add the strip of shops and offices along Bird Road and you get many buildings with flat roofs, parapets and rooftop equipment. A COI goes to the facilities office or the property manager ahead of time, and the visit can be timed for when classes or services are out. Details for managers are on the HOA and commercial page.

What a visit to a Westchester home looks like

  1. Reading your picture

    Your snapshot of the traffic at the hole usually tells us whether the job is a ground box, a soffit or an opened wall, and the quote follows.

  2. Parking and neighbors

    If you have a driveway, the truck parks there and stays off the swale. If the work faces a neighbor's yard, a quick word to them beforehand keeps kids and pets inside.

  3. Finding and opening the cavity

    A thermal scan marks where the comb is. Only that section gets opened. A gentle low-suction vac draws the bees into a screened carrier, then the comb and honey come out by hand.

  4. Closing up

    The space is cleaned, the entry is sealed, and the wall, soffit or tile is put back by our own trades. That patch is covered by our workmanship warranty.

Questions people ask us

Westchester isn't a city. Does that change anything about getting bees out of my house?

Not for your own property. Westchester is unincorporated, with Miami-Dade County as its government rather than a city hall, but a colony in your wall, roof or yard is handled the same way anywhere from Tamiami Trail down to Bird Road. You call, send a photo, and get a quote.

My house is from the 1950s. Will taking out a hive mean ripping out a big piece of wall?

Usually not. The thermal scan shows where the colony is before anything is opened, so the cut is limited to the area holding comb. Our own contractors then patch the block, stucco or drywall and paint it to match, and you can ask for photos of each stage.

Is a colony in the sprinkler valve box by the swale cheaper to deal with than one in a wall?

Usually, because nothing on the house gets opened. Ground boxes are easy to reach and do not need anything rebuilt, so they usually fall in the roughly $300 to $400 range. Bees that have moved behind stucco or beneath tile push the price up, since something has to be opened and then rebuilt.

Can you just kill the bees so it's over faster?

That is not something we do; the bees leave alive and settle with a local beekeeper. Killing bees inside a wall also backfires: the dead brood and leftover honey rot, draw pests, and can soak through drywall. Removing the bees and all the comb is the job that actually ends the problem.

I manage a church building near Coral Way. Can you work with our insurance requirements?

Yes. Tell us who on your board or staff keeps insurance paperwork and the COI will be in their inbox before anyone climbs a ladder. Work can be booked for a weekday when the sanctuary and classrooms sit empty. An itemized invoice and photos are available for your records.

A crew patched our addition wall after a removal. Who pays if a new swarm targets that patch?

Not you. Our repair work carries a warranty. Should a new colony try the same patched opening, a crew returns to that exact place and handles it. Bees moving into a different gap elsewhere on the house is a separate problem, which the quote will explain.
