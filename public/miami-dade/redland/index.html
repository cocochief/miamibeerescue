Redland farm country

Redland bee removal across barns, groves and shade houses

Five-acre lots, coral rock walls, pump houses and machinery that sits idle between seasons give a wandering swarm plenty of choices. This page walks through getting an unwanted colony off your acreage alive.

Quick answer

If a wild honey bee colony has moved into a building, shed, piece of equipment or tree on your Redland property, it can be taken out alive and given to a beekeeper, with the comb removed and the opening sealed so a new swarm is not drawn back. Hive boxes kept by a beekeeper are a different matter: those bees have an owner, and the owner is who you call. For a wild colony, call or text us a photo any hour; the quote costs nothing.

If anyone is being stung

Walk away in a straight line, toward a closed vehicle or building, the moment bees pour out of a barn wall or a pot stack or start stinging someone. Do not swat, spray or run the tractor near them. Call 911 for anyone with swelling around the face or trouble breathing, then call us; stung people are handled first.

Around Redland

  • StatusUnincorporated farm area; Miami-Dade County is the local government
  • Named forThe rust-colored clay found in pockets atop the oolite
  • Rock plowingThe first tractor able to scarify rock land, 1914
  • ZoningAgricultural zoning: houses on at least five acres
  • Fruit & Spice ParkA 37-acre park run by Miami-Dade County

Redland has never been a city. It is an unincorporated stretch of groves, nurseries and five-acre homesteads that Miami-Dade County governs directly, and even its edges depend on whom you ask. The name comes from the pockets of red clay sitting on oolitic limestone, rock so hard that early growers dynamited holes in it to plant trees, until a tractor built to scarify rock land started grinding it flat in 1914.

That history still shapes where bees end up. A property here is rarely one house and a lawn. It is a farmhouse, a tool shed, a pump house, a shade house, stacks of empty nursery pots, a parked disc harrow and a row of mature avocado or mango trees, often with no one walking past most of them for weeks. A scout bee looking for a dry, dark cavity has a lot to inspect, and a colony can be well built before anyone notices. Some properties also carry managed hives that belong to a beekeeper. Those stay. This page is about the uninvited colonies in your structures, gear and trees, and how to get them out without killing them.

Where a wild colony settles on a Redland property

Shade houses and potting sheds

Nurseries keep shade cloth frames, stacked containers, pallets and potting benches that stay undisturbed for long stretches. A swarm can fill the hollow of a stacked pot tower or the space under a bench. Work around the plants is slow and careful so the stock is not trampled. See nursery and grove removals.

Pump houses and irrigation boxes

Grove irrigation means pump enclosures, well heads and valve boxes scattered across the acreage. A small entry hole, shade and a nearby water source make these attractive. The colony comes out with the comb, and the lid or vent gets screened. The utility box removal page has more on these enclosures.

Barns, tool sheds and equipment

A tractor hood, a toolbox on a flatbed, an old sprayer tank or the wall of a pole barn can hold comb by the time the season turns. Equipment is opened up, the comb is cut out and the parts are cleaned before you need them. See sheds and outbuildings.

Coral rock walls

Oolite walls are part of the Redland landscape, and wherever stones have loosened or a joint has opened, a colony can tuck into the void behind the face. Removal means lifting stones by hand and setting them back, then packing the opening so the cavity cannot be reused.

Mature grove trees

Old avocado and mango trees develop hollow limbs and trunk cavities, and storm damage opens more. Because the tree is still alive, the aim is to reach the comb through one cut panel in the trunk or limb and close that panel up again once the bees are out. Details on palm and tree colonies.

Old clapboard farmhouses

Some early settlers' clapboard homes still stand, and a gap at a corner board or gable vent leads straight into a wall bay. Before a single board is pried loose, a thermal camera points to the bay holding the heat of the brood.

Crossroads, old sections and what they mean for access

  • Redland Road and Bauer Drive

    The old center of the community, where the 1912 Pioneer Guild Hall still stands as the Redland Grocery. Nearby properties mix older wood buildings with newer barns, so the first step is figuring out which structure the bees are using and where the entry sits.

  • Silver Palm

    Around Silver Palm Drive and Newton Road stand the 1904 Silver Palm Schoolhouse and the 1911 Anderson general store, both on the National Register. On historic wood buildings, the aim is to open as little original material as possible and match what comes off.

  • Aladdin City section

    Near Hainlin Mill Drive and Farm Life School Road, a planned community started in 1926 left behind a few Arabian-themed street names. Tell us the cross street when you text, since GPS pins out here can land a lot away.

  • Fruit & Spice Park area

    Grove and nursery land surrounds the county's 37-acre fruit park. Properties are deep, so directions to the right outbuilding, plus notes on dogs or livestock, save time on arrival.

  • Krome Avenue frontage

    The state began widening Krome Avenue to four lanes through Redland in 2019. Farm stands and supply buildings close to the road need a safe place to park the truck off the shoulder, and work near busy traffic is set up with that in mind.

  • Newton Road south of Bauer Drive

    The former Orchid Jungle site here, destroyed by Hurricane Andrew, is now the Hattie Bauer Hammock Preserve. Where properties sit near hammock and heavy tree cover, a limb that comes down in a storm can reveal comb nobody knew about.

Whose bees are these?

Out here a lot of people see honey bees every day and never think twice, because pollination and honey are part of farm life. Florida law requires beekeepers to register with the state agriculture department, and state apiary inspectors monitor colonies for diseases, pests and unwanted bee species. If the bees are coming and going from white hive boxes on a neighbor's land, they are someone's livestock, and the beekeeper is the right first call.

The trouble starts when a swarm leaves a hive, wild or managed, and moves into something you use. Once comb is built inside your barn wall, your pump enclosure or your parked mower, the colony is wild for practical purposes and it is yours to deal with. Those colonies get removed live and handed to a beekeeper who wants them, not sprayed. Unsure which of the two is on your land? Keep well back, zoom in on whatever the traffic is pouring into, and send that image; it usually answers the question. Bees bunched on a branch without any comb are a swarm at rest, a temporary stop on the way to a permanent home.

Working around crops, crews and stock

A grove or nursery is a workplace, and a removal has to fit into the day. No pesticides are used on the colony, which matters when the bees are sitting a few feet from plants headed to market. The bees are vacuumed or boxed and carried off, the comb is cut out, and leftover honey gets wiped up before it can sour and invite ants or a fresh swarm.

Tell us when crews are spraying, harvesting or moving stock so the job does not land in the middle of it. If the colony is inside a structure, thermal imaging maps the warm cluster first, so a shed wall or barn panel is opened only where the comb is. When boards or siding have to come off, our own licensed contractors put them back. The relocation and repair pages explain both halves in more detail, and the thermal imaging guide shows what the camera can and cannot see.

After a storm rolls through

Redland has a long record of hurricane damage to groves, nurseries and buildings, and Hurricane Andrew in 1992 destroyed landmarks such as Orchid Jungle. A storm does two things to bees. It tears open trees and sheds where colonies were already living, leaving comb exposed and bees agitated, and it creates fresh cavities that swarms move into later. Walk the property slowly after a blow and keep people and dogs away from any split trunk or peeled-back roof panel with bees around it. Before touching any downed limb, read what to check when wind exposes a colony. If bees keep returning to a spot that was cleaned out years ago, the old comb scent is often the reason, which why bees come back explains.

What a farm-property job costs

A colony sitting low and easy to reach, such as a valve box or a pot stack, generally runs about $300 to $400. Comb hidden inside a barn wall, a roof or a hollow tree that needs cutting and rebuilding reaches into the thousands when carpentry and roofing are part of the bill. If the bees are tucked in a back shed and bothering nobody, holding off until Monday keeps the bill down, because after-hours emergency calls are billed higher. For farm bookkeeping or an insurance file, ask and you will get pictures of the job plus an itemized bill, and commercial nurseries can request a certificate of insurance. The cost page breaks the price drivers down further.

From the farm gate to the sealed opening

  1. Directions first

    Send the address, the gate code or who will open the gate, and which building or tree has the bees. Out here a driveway can run a long way, and a pin dropped at the right shed helps.

  2. Walk the property together

    Point out the entry, any dogs, horses or goats, and when irrigation or spraying is scheduled. Animals get moved or penned before the bees are disturbed.

  3. Find the comb, then open up

    Thermal imaging shows the cluster inside walls, equipment or trees. Only that section is opened, and the bees are collected alive for a beekeeper.

  4. Clean, seal and stand behind it

    Every scrap of wax leaves with the crew and the hole is shut tight. The warranty covers that seal: if bees show up at it again, we drive back out.

Questions people ask us

A beekeeper keeps hive boxes on the farm next to mine. Will you take those away?

No. Boxed colonies kept for honey or pollination are someone's property, and Florida requires their keeper to register with the state. If those bees are bothering you, talk with the beekeeper or the landowner first. What we handle is a wild colony that has moved into your building, equipment or trees, whether it started as a swarm from a managed hive or from the wild.

Bees built comb inside a tractor I parked for the summer. Can it be saved?

Usually, yes. Panels and covers are opened, the bees are collected alive, and the comb and honey are cut out and cleaned so nothing sticky is left on belts, wiring or the seat. Tell us the make and where the bees enter so the right tools come along.

I grow for market. Will anything be sprayed near my plants?

No pesticide is used on the colony. The bees are vacuumed or boxed and taken to a beekeeper, and the comb is physically removed. Let us know about any spraying or harvest on your schedule so the visit stays out of your crew's way.

Is Redland part of Homestead, and does that change anything?

Redland is unincorporated, so Miami-Dade County is the local government rather than a city hall. For a bee removal that makes little practical difference. What matters more is gate access, the size of the lot and which structure the colony is in.

There's a swarm hanging in my mango tree. Should I wait?

A bare cluster of bees on a limb is a waiting party; when the scouting is done the cluster departs together, frequently by the next day. Keep people and animals back and watch it. If it is near a path, a door or a pump house, call us and it can be picked up before it moves into something you own.
