Quick answer

Hialeah Gardens is built mostly at one story, so a colony in a house eave or in the hood over a warehouse roll-up door is often within ladder reach, no lift required. Low, open nests like that tend to land in the $300 to $400 band; anything that means opening a wall or roof and rebuilding it runs into the thousands. Whichever it is, nothing gets sprayed and the bees are relocated by a beekeeper. You pay nothing for the quote, and a 3 a.m. call is picked up and covered by the same 24-hour response guarantee as one at noon.

If anyone is being stung

A worker or neighbor taking stings should be moved behind a closed door or into a truck cab with the windows up. Swinging at the cloud or soaking it with a hose only makes it angrier. If lips or throat puff up, or breathing turns hard, dial 911 before you dial us. In a warehouse, drop the nearest bay door. Then phone the line; a sting emergency outranks every routine job, whatever the hour.

Hialeah Gardens fits two very different kinds of property into 3.67 square miles. It lies northwest of downtown Miami, with Hialeah wrapped around its north and east sides and Medley across the canal to the southwest. The city's zoning maps divide it into four districts: the north is mostly single-family homes and estate lots, the northwest is given over to industry, and the central and south districts mix housing with shops and offices. A honey bee colony hunting for a dry, dark cavity pays no attention to that line. It may choose the soffit of a one-story house near the water, or the hollow cap above a roll-up door in a yard where a roofing company keeps its stock.

The removal follows the same principle on both sides and plays out differently on each. At a house, the talk is about your kids, your eaves and how the hole gets closed afterward. In the industrial district, it is about keeping trucks moving, keeping workers out of the flight path and giving the owner or manager the paperwork they need. The notes below go district by district, so you know what to expect before anyone sets up a ladder.

Where colonies settle in Hialeah Gardens

Eaves on one-story houses

Most of the city is built low, so colonies often end up behind a soffit vent or where the fascia board has pulled away from the roofline. Those entries are usually within reach of a standard ladder, which keeps the job nearer the ground-level price and the patch small.

Warehouse walls and door housings

The northwest district is lined with warehouses and workshops. Bees slip into the hood over a roll-up door, a seam where wall panels meet, or an open block cell behind cracked stucco. Before any panel is unbolted, a heat-sensing camera traces the outline of the nest from outside.

Stacked materials in contractor yards

Roofing, cement and construction businesses keep pallets, tile bundles, forms and spare equipment outdoors. A pile left alone for a few weeks offers a swarm a sheltered cavity, and the colony can be cut out without dumping the whole stack.

Parked trailers and idle equipment

Truck and trailer dealers and shipping companies operate here. A trailer, toolbox or generator housing that sits for a season is an inviting box for scout bees. Look before you hitch up or crank an engine, because jolting the unit can send the whole colony out.

Trees near the water and the canal

Roughly an eighth of the city's area is water, and the Miami Canal runs along the Medley side. Bees carry water home to cool the brood, so hollow trunks and thick crowns near the banks are natural candidates for both resting swarms and settled colonies.

District by district

  • North district

    Low- to moderate-density single-family homes share this part of the city with estate-zoned lots. Bigger parcels mean sheds, detached garages and tree lines along the back fence, all places a colony can build for months without anyone noticing.

  • Northwest district

    The industrial core, home to shipping and moving companies, roofing and construction firms, truck and trailer sales and some food manufacturers. Expect gates, forklifts and a working schedule. Tell us when deliveries stop and the visit is planned around them.

  • Central district

    Moderate- and medium-density housing sits next to general business uses. Where homes share walls or a roof, a colony in one unit's eave can become the neighbor's problem too, and the go-ahead may have to come from an association or a landlord.

  • South district

    High-density housing, commercial strips and some industry meet here, crossed east to west by NW 103rd Street. With so many owners side by side, settle who is responsible for the wall before work starts; the renter and landlord guide helps.

  • US 27 commercial strip

    Most of the city's commercial businesses line the east side of US 27, which forms much of the western boundary and has a parallel frontage road. Sign boxes and roof edges here face heavy traffic, so a ladder setup needs cones and a clear plan.

Workshops, bay doors and a crew on the clock

A colony inside a workshop wall is a different job from one in a bedroom soffit, mostly because of everything happening around it. Forklifts, open bay doors and crews on the loading apron all sit inside the bees' flight path, so the first step is closing off a work zone and agreeing on timing with whoever runs the floor.

Because the work is licensed and insured, a COI can reach the yard owner, landlord or manager ahead of the visit, made out to whatever name their file requires. Our page on commercial and property manager jobs explains how that paperwork works. Night and weekend visits are available if the yard cannot stop during the day, though they cost more than a weekday call. Ask and you get photos plus a line-by-line invoice for the maintenance file, handy when a leased building's tenant and owner must agree on what was done. For colonies deep in block or panel walls, see bees inside walls, and the cost page lays out what pushes a commercial job above the ground-level range. A company that also keeps yards in Doral, Miami Springs, Miami Lakes or out in West Kendall can line up every site through the same call.

A small city that grew quickly

Hialeah Gardens started as the Walter C. Ohlerts Tourist Camp and incorporated as a town in December 1948. Its first building code followed in February 1949. For years it was rural, and raising horses was one of its main industries, until a 1968 land use and zoning master plan set off growth that made it one of the faster growing municipalities in the county.

What that means for bees today is mostly about height. The city is largely single-story, so entry holes are often reachable without a lift, and many jobs stay close to the $300 to $400 range. The catch is that low eaves sit right beside doors, windows and walkways, so a colony there gets bumped often and can turn defensive. Once the comb is out, sealing matters as much as removal, because wax and honey residue keep calling new scouts back. The guide on returning bees covers that lingering lure, and soffit and eave removal covers how that opening gets rebuilt, with the carpentry, roofing and paint handled by licensed contractors on our own crew.

Water, the canal and swarm season

About 0.45 of the city's 3.67 square miles is water, and the Miami Canal marks the line with Medley. Water is something every colony needs, because foragers haul it home to cool the nest in summer heat. Spring is the main swarm season, though in this climate a swarm can show up in any month.

A swarm clustered on a branch by the water is resting while scouts look for a permanent home. If they settle on your soffit or a cracked fascia board, it stops being a swarm and becomes a colony that builds comb. A handful of bees probing the same gap day after day is a warning sign; the scout bee guide explains it, and calling before they commit is cheaper. Colonies already inside hollow trunks near the banks are handled through tree and palm removal. After a strong storm, check fallen limbs and torn screens too; the storm guide shows what an exposed colony looks like.

How a Hialeah Gardens visit runs

  1. Photo and triage

    Snap the opening from a safe distance and mention whether it is your house, a rented unit or a commercial yard. A free quote comes back, and anyone being stung jumps the queue.

  2. Gate, parking and people

    For yards in the northwest district we ask for the gate contact, a spot for the truck off the frontage road and a window when forklifts and deliveries pause, so nobody walks into the bees.

  3. Find, remove, relocate

    Comb and bees leave together, cut out by hand once a heat scan has marked the cavity. The colony rides off boxed for a beekeeper, and leftover honey is wiped out so its smell does not draw a new swarm.

  4. Close up and paperwork

    Roofers, painters and licensed contractors who work directly for us rebuild the gap to match. Should bees ever push back into a seal we made, the warrantied workmanship means another trip out. Photos and an itemized bill come with it if your landlord wants them.

Questions people ask us

Bees are coming out above our warehouse roll-up door. Do we have to shut down for the day?

Not necessarily. The area around that door is closed off while the colony comes out, and the rest of the building can keep working. If the only safe window is a night or a weekend, that can be arranged, though after-hours visits cost more than a weekday one. Have your shipping schedule handy when you call so the work zone can be planned around it.

Our property manager wants proof of insurance before anyone works on the building. Is that possible?

Yes. A COI naming the owner, landlord, HOA or management company can be issued ahead of the visit. Give us the exact legal name to list and the address or inbox it should go to, and the document will be in their hands before the crew pulls up to the gate.

Bees are bunched on a branch over the water behind my yard. Should I wait for them to go?

Waiting can work. A cluster like that is a swarm resting while scouts decide where to live, and it often lifts off by the next morning or within two or three days. The trouble starts if the spot they choose is your fascia or a hollow wall. Keep pets and people back, skip the spray, and use swarm pickup if you want it gone before it moves in.

My landlord owns the house and bees moved into a wall. Do I arrange the removal?

Let your landlord know in writing right away, with a photo if you can take one from a safe spot. Either of you can request the removal, but the owner normally decides on repairs. The tenant and landlord guide lays out who handles which part.

Is there any case where the bees get exterminated instead of moved?

No. Removal here is live and humane every time, and the colony goes to a beekeeper. A hard spot means more work opening and rebuilding the structure, which shows up in the price, not a can of pesticide. Thermal imaging helps keep the opening as small as possible.
