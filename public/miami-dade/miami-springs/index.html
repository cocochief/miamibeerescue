Quick answer

Pueblo Revival houses here wear flat roofs ringed by parapets, so a colony in a Miami Springs home often sits between that low wall and the roof deck, where the opening can be made from above and the street face stays untouched. Around Miami Springs

Glenn Curtiss and James Bright developed Miami Springs during the 1920s land boom under the name Country Club Estates, and its signature look, Pueblo Revival, was borrowed from adobe country: stuccoed walls, rounded corners and flat roofs ringed by parapets. The Curtiss Mansion on Deer Run and the old Hotel Country Club, later known as Fair Havens, were both built in that style. That style is handsome from the sidewalk and, for a honey bee scout, full of possibilities. A flat roof behind a parapet hides voids nobody sees from the street, and wherever a wooden beam end pokes through stucco, the joint around it loosens over the decades.

The rest of the city fills in around a golf course that opened in 1923, with the airport along the south edge and a canal along the north and east. That is a lot of open ground and nearby water for foraging bees, packed into under three square miles. When a colony picks your house, the job is to take it out alive, close the hole properly and leave the building looking the way it did before.

Where colonies settle on Miami Springs buildings

Behind flat-roof parapets

A Pueblo Revival roofline hides a low wall around a flat deck. Where the parapet meets the roof, flashing lifts and small voids open up. Bees use them as a doorway into a dark, dry pocket that stays out of sight until the comb grows and honey starts to stain the stucco below.

Around projecting beam ends

Some buildings in this style carry wooden beam ends, called vigas, that stick out through the wall. Wood and stucco move differently with heat and moisture, so the seam around each beam widens a little every year. A slit you could barely slide a house key into is wide enough for a colony.

Inside thick stucco walls

Thick, sloped walls look solid, but the space behind the stucco can hold a full colony. Bees keep their brood close to 95 degrees, and that warm patch shows up on a thermal camera, which tells the crew where to open the wall instead of cutting long lines through old plaster.

Meter and irrigation boxes

A buried valve box or a meter housing in the yard is a ready-made cavity at ground level. Whoever lifts the lid next gets the surprise. If you find bees in one, keep people and mowers away, put the lid back only if you can do it without disturbing them, and call.

Coral rock walls and planters

If your yard has coral rock (oolitic limestone) walls or planters, the porous stone and crumbling mortar leave small cavities close to the ground. The Curtiss Mansion's own barbecue grill was built from rock dug out when its lake was made.

Sheds and garage rafters

A detached shed or a garage attic that nobody opens for weeks is about as close to a hollow tree as a city lot offers. Before reaching behind stored boxes or up into the rafters, look and listen for a steady hum and traffic at a gap.

    Around Glenn H. Curtiss Memorial Circle, a swarm hanging from a sign or a branch draws attention quickly. Keep people back and call. That cluster is waiting on its scouts to choose a cavity. If bees begin streaming into a nearby wall or roof edge instead, the colony has picked a home.

    The pueblo-style building that opened as the Hotel Country Club and later became Fair Havens was designated a Miami Springs Historic Site in 1984. On any historic property, plan for small, careful openings and patching that matches the original stucco texture and paint.

    The Miami River canal runs along the north and east boundary, with Hialeah across the water and U.S. 27 running beside that border. Bees need water every day, and a colony living near this edge never has far to fly for it.

Getting a hive out of a 1920s Pueblo Revival house

Older stucco does not forgive careless cutting. The oldest Pueblo Revival walls in this city date to the land boom, and patches made with the wrong mix or texture stand out for years. So the work starts with a thermal scan and a look at where the bees are actually entering, which can be several feet from where the comb sits. The goal is one opening, sized to the comb, in the place that is easiest to put back.

On a flat roof, the opening may be made from the deck side behind the parapet, so the face of the house is never touched. Either way, every piece of comb and honey comes out, since leftover honey ferments, drips and draws new bees. After that, the company's own roofers, painters and licensed contractors rebuild the patch so it disappears into the wall. The details are on the pages for tile and flat roof hives and bees inside block and frame walls, and what thermal imaging shows explains the camera work. Should bees ever try that sealed patch again, the crew returns, because the workmanship carries a warranty.

Swarms, scouts and a city with lots of open ground

A ball of bees hanging from a branch is a swarm, a colony in transit with no comb to defend. It is usually calm and usually temporary. Meanwhile, scout bees spend a few days checking cavities, and a parapet void or a gap around a beam end can win. Notice a few bees poking at one gap in the stucco day after day? That is the scouting phase, so read up on scout bees checking out a house and seal small gaps before a swarm commits.

When a steady stream of bees uses the same opening, comb is already under construction, and it needs to come out rather than be waited out. A swarm caught early is a simpler job; see swarm pickup. Bees that have already moved in call for live honey bee removal.

How a visit runs in Miami Springs

  1. Photo and call first

    Send a photo of the entry and say whether it is a flat roof, a parapet, a wall, a beam end or a yard box. That decides who comes out and whether the job needs roof access.

  2. Thermal scan before cutting

    On site, the tech watches the flight path, then scans the wall or roof deck with a thermal camera to find the edges of the comb, so any opening in old stucco stays as small as it can.

  3. Live removal and hand-off

    Comb comes out piece by piece, the bees are gathered with a gentle vacuum, the queen with them when she can be found, and the whole colony leaves in a box bound for a beekeeper's yard.

  4. Sealing, repair and records

    Every scrap of honey and wax is scraped out, the bees' doorway gets closed for good, and stucco or roof patching follows. If a landlord, buyer or board needs proof, ask, and pictures of each stage plus an invoice listing every charge come with the job.

Questions people ask us

My house has a flat roof with a parapet. Can bees really get in up there?

Yes. The joint where a parapet meets a flat roof is one of the easier ways into a dry, hidden space. Watch the top of the wall for bees going in and out at one point. If you see steady traffic, a colony is probably settled between the parapet and the roof deck, and removal is usually done from the roof side so the front of the house stays untouched.

Will taking out a hive ruin the original stucco on an older Miami Springs home?

It should not, if the opening is planned. Scanning the wall first with a heat camera maps the colony, which lets the crew make a single cut sized to the comb. Our own licensed contractors and painters patch the opening to match the texture and color. On a historic property, say so up front so the repair can be planned around the original finish.

Is a bee cluster on a branch by the golf course an emergency?

Usually not. Bees bunched on a limb have built no comb yet. They are resting in transit until scouts settle on a cavity, and plenty of these clusters lift off within a day or two. Give it a wide berth, leave the hose and the spray can in the garage, and watch where it heads. Call if it hangs over a door, a walkway or a play area, stays past a couple of days, or starts filing into a wall or roof edge nearby.

If I hit the parapet gap with insecticide tonight, is the problem solved?

No, and please skip it. Spray stirs up the colony and puts anyone nearby at risk, and it does not remove anything. A dead colony leaves its comb and honey in the wall, where the honey ferments, leaks through the stucco and draws new bees and pests. Live removal takes the comb out with the bees, and they go to a beekeeper.

Someone just got stung after dark on a Sunday. Will anyone pick up?

A real person answers whatever the hour, the 24-hour response guarantee covers every call, and a household with someone stung moves to the front. Emergency visits after hours or on a Saturday or Sunday are priced higher than weekday work, so when the colony is quiet and nobody is in its path, waiting for a weekday saves money.

Our business near the airport needs insurance paperwork. Can you provide it?

A certificate of insurance can go out before the visit, made out to your company, the building owner or the management firm, because the work is licensed and insured. Once the comb is out, you can also ask for photos and an invoice that breaks out each item.
