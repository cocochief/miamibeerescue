Postwar soffits, block cores and canal lots: North Miami bee colonies

North Miami turned one hundred in 2026, and plenty of its houses are old enough to have settled, cracked and opened up. Honey bees notice those openings long before an owner does.

Quick answer

Much of North Miami went up in the post-1945 boom, so colonies here tend to hide inside soffits, attic vents and hollow block walls. A steady line of bees at one gap, hour after hour, means comb behind it. Keep kids and pets away, and leave that gap open and unsprayed; sealed-in bees can come indoors. Call or text a picture. We cut into the cavity, hand the whole colony and its wax to a beekeeper, then close the wall. Low, reachable jobs run about $300 to $400; high walls, roofs and repairs cost more.

If anyone is being stung

Anyone getting stung should get inside a closed room or a car first, because running in the open keeps the bees chasing. Skip the tweezers; drag stingers off the skin with a fingernail or card edge. Dial 911 if lips or tongue swell, breathing gets hard, or the person has a bee allergy. Then phone us. If someone is hurt, say so; that job goes first, at 2 a.m. or noon.

Around North Miami

  • IncorporatedFebruary 5, 1926, first named the Town of Miami Shores
  • Became a cityMay 27, 1953, under a new charter
  • Population60,191 at the 2020 census
  • Land and waterWater covers just over 15% of the city's 10.05 sq mi
  • Early nameArch Creek, after a natural limestone bridge that fell in 1973

Before it had a town charter, this stretch of north Dade was known as Arch Creek, named for a forty-foot limestone bridge over the water. The bridge collapsed in 1973, but water still shapes the city: the bayfront on one side and the Biscayne Canal, dug in 1924, cutting through. For a colony, that canal is a supply line. Bees haul water home daily to cool the brood and thin stored honey, so foragers set up regular trips to pool edges, birdbaths, canal banks and dripping hose bibs.

The other half of the picture is housing. After 1945, returning veterans and young families poured in, and by 1951 North Miami was counted among the fastest-growing towns in the country. Houses from that boom are now around seventy years old, and seventy years of sun, roof work, rewiring and new AC lines leave small gaps in soffits, block walls and attic vents. A scout bee can squeeze through a gap narrower than a pencil and decide your house is worth a look.

Where colonies settle on North Miami property

Soffits on postwar houses

Wood or vented soffits from the city's postwar building wave often have loose panels or rotted corners. Behind them is a dry, shaded box that holds a steady temperature, close to ideal for comb. The tell is a tight knot of bees coming and going where a panel has pulled away.

Hollow cores in block walls

Inside a concrete block wall are vertical hollow cores, one stacked over the next. A missing grout joint, an old pipe hole or a gap around a hose bib lets bees into one core, and the comb then grows up and down inside it. A low hum through the drywall can be the first sign, before any bees turn up indoors.

Meter, valve and sprinkler boxes

Ground boxes for water meters, irrigation valves and backflow devices are in almost every front yard. Shaded, damp and seldom checked, they let a colony grow quietly until a meter reader or sprinkler tech pops the cover.

Palms and old shade trees

Mature yards in the older sections hold palms with dead frond bases and hardwoods with hollow limbs. Either can hold a hive, and tree jobs need care so the tree survives the removal.

Docks, seawalls and boat storage

Along the bay and the canal banks, dock boxes, covered boat lifts and storage under decks give bees shelter a few steps from fresh water. Those spots are tight to work, so access gets planned before the visit.

Sheds and detached garages

Tool sheds and garage attics on deep older lots go untouched for long stretches. Colonies move into the rafters or wall gaps, and the first sign is often bees at a vent.

Old seams, new gaps: why these houses attract colonies

A Mediterranean-style city hall went up in 1928, and the William Jennings Bryan school followed the same year. The big wave came after 1945, when veterans and their young families arrived in large numbers. Then came additions, enclosed porches, new roofs and modern AC equipment bolted onto old walls.

Each of those layers can leave a seam where two materials meet. Honey bees look for a dry cavity with one small, defensible entrance, and a seam behind stucco or a gap where a new line passes through an old wall fits that description well. That is why a colony can show up on a house that has never had bees before. Nothing changed for you, but the right gap finally met the right swarm.

If you are not sure whether you have a colony or just a passing cluster, our guide to scout bees checking out a house explains the difference, and our page on swarm pickup covers the ball of bees that rests on a shrub or mailbox for a few hours before moving on. Hidden comb that nobody can see is easier to map with a thermal camera.

Water on every side of the map

Roughly fifteen percent of North Miami is water. The city fronts Biscayne Bay, and the Biscayne Canal was dug in 1924 to drain the land. A colony needs water every day, and foragers will drink from anything reliable, including canal banks, pool steps and fountains.

Bees at your pool do not always mean a hive on your property. Foragers can fly a mile or more. Still, when the crowd at the water holds steady for days and a flight line leads to a crack nearby, the nest is likely close. On waterfront lots we also check docks and seawall gaps, covered on our page for docks, seawalls and boats, and the pump housings and screen frames covered in pool enclosures and equipment.

The campus, the museum and the park

Three large properties in and around North Miami are run by bigger institutions. Florida International University's Biscayne Bay Campus covers about 200 acres on the bay, next to Oleta River State Park. The Museum of Contemporary Art is at 770 NE 125th Street. Arch Creek Park, a hammock and archaeological site of about nine acres, is run by Miami-Dade County Parks.

If you see bees on those grounds, report it to that property's own staff, since they control the land. If the colony is in your building, your condo or your business, that is where we come in. For associations and offices we can provide certificates of insurance, and our HOA and commercial page explains how we work with managers. Two bordering cities have pages too, North Miami Beach to the north and Sunny Isles Beach to the east. Miami Shores lies just south of the village of Biscayne Park, with the City of Miami beyond it, and Aventura is another North Dade page. The full list is on our Miami-Dade service map.

Buying, selling or renting an older North Miami house

On a house from the postwar years, a home inspection can turn up old comb stains, a sealed-over hive or live bees in a wall. If that happens during a sale, our guide to bees found during a home inspection walks through what buyers and sellers usually need. Tenants and landlords sharing one house can use our guide to renting with bees to sort out who calls whom.

An empty wall cavity still holding wax is unfinished work. Leftover comb and honey pull in new swarms and can attract pests, so the full job includes taking out every piece of comb and then sealing the entry. Our page on comb and honey cleanup explains that step, and what draws bees back to a sealed wall explains what goes wrong when it is skipped.

How a North Miami visit runs

  1. Gate and parking

    If you live behind the Sans Souci guardhouse or another gated entry, give the guard our name. On narrow residential streets we park so the driveway and neighbors stay clear.

  2. Heat-map the hidden comb

    The flight path tells us where to aim. A thermal scan then shows how far the comb spreads behind stucco, block or soffit, so nothing is opened blind.

  3. Remove it alive

    We open the cavity, take the bees and every piece of comb, and keep the colony together for a beekeeper. Nothing is sprayed to kill them.

  4. Close the wall, patch the finish

    With the cavity emptied, the hole is closed and the finish patched by the licensed tradespeople we employ: contractors, roofers and painters. If you need paperwork, the job comes with pictures and a line-by-line invoice on request.

Questions people ask us

My North Miami house was built in the 1950s. Why are bees showing up now?

Age opens gaps slowly. A soffit panel loosens, a grout joint wears, or an old pipe hole loses its seal. Bees only need one opening into a dry cavity. When a swarm happens to pass while that gap is open, scouts can find it, and the colony may move in within a day or two.

Bees are drinking at my pool next to the canal. Do I have a hive?

Not always. Foragers will travel a long way for steady water, and the canal side of a yard is a natural stop. If the numbers stay high for a week, follow them with your eyes. Bees heading straight to a vent, a roof edge or a gap in stucco are pointing at home.

I live in Sans Souci. Do I have to tell the guardhouse you're coming?

Yes, it helps a lot. Give the guard our name, or send us the visitor steps when you book. That keeps the truck and equipment moving straight to your house instead of waiting at the gate.

Is a North Miami colony exterminated or relocated?

Relocated, always. Nothing gets exterminated here. A beekeeper takes the colony, wax and all, once it is out of your wall, and we clean and close the empty space behind it. Exterminating a hive in a wall also leaves rotting comb and honey behind, which brings new problems.

What does a removal from a block wall usually cost?

A low hive in an easy-to-reach wall usually falls around $300 to $400. When the comb sits high, spans several block cores or needs a roofer afterward, the bill climbs, sometimes into the thousands. Quotes are free, and after-hours or weekend emergency calls carry a higher rate than a regular weekday booking.

A colony turned up again where your crew closed the wall. Is that covered?

Our workmanship carries a warranty. If a new colony turns up in that same sealed spot, phone us and a crew heads back out to fix it. A comeback can point to a second gap nearby or wax left in the cavity, which is why every scrap of comb comes out the first time.
