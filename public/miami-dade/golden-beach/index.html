Quick answer

Every vehicle entering Golden Beach comes off State Road A1A past a police guardhouse, so give the guards or your house manager our name and a time before the visit. Then text a close-up of the entry hole or call. No poison is sprayed; the bees leave living, boxed for relocation. Ground-level swarms and hives typically run $300 to $400; comb behind stucco or under roof tile costs more because our own licensed tradespeople rebuild the opening. Someone answers at 3 a.m., and the 24-hour response guarantee reaches this town like anywhere in the county.

If anyone is being stung

Bees chasing and stinging? Walk fast to the nearest house or vehicle, shut yourself in, and pull stingers out by scraping. Do not jump into the pool or the Intracoastal to escape; bees wait at the surface. Call 911 for trouble breathing or many stings, then call us; emergency removal puts you at the front of the line.

Around Golden Beach

  • IncorporatedMay 23, 1929, by special act of the Florida Legislature
  • SizeAbout one mile north to south and four blocks across
  • HousingSingle-family homes only; the charter bars commercial use and high-rise condos
  • BeachA mile and one-eighth of private beach for residents and their guests
  • Getting inOne road in from State Road A1A, past a police guardhouse

Golden Beach runs only about a mile from end to end and is private enough that every visitor is noticed. The town has a single road in from State Road A1A, a police guardhouse at that entrance, and a charter that keeps out shops, hotels and condo towers. What fills the mile between the county line and its southern edge is houses, most of them on the ocean, the Intracoastal or one of the canals. Its only Miami-Dade neighbors are Sunny Isles Beach to the south and Aventura across the Intracoastal to the west.

For a honey bee scout, that is a lot of good real estate. Large homes have long rooflines, deep soffits, hollow columns and equipment rooms, and a house left closed for a few weeks gives a colony time to settle in. Waterfront lots add dock boxes, seawall gaps and pump housings. Salt air and sun break down caulk and screen faster here than they do inland, so small openings appear on their own. On a house that big, nobody notices when one of those cracks becomes a doorway. Once comb goes up behind a wall, the colony will not leave without help, and the job turns from a pickup into a careful opening, removal and rebuild.

The cavities a scout picks on an estate lot

Roof tile and the eave line

Barrel and flat tile leave hollow channels at the ridge and along the eaves. A colony can build comb in that space for months before anyone notices more than a steady line of bees at one corner of the roof. Our tile roof hive page covers the lift-and-reset work.

Soffits on two-story houses

Tall eaves with vented soffit panels are hard to inspect from the ground. A loose panel or a gap where the soffit meets the wall gives bees a dry, shaded cavity well above the pool deck, the classic case for soffit and eave removal.

Docks, lifts and seawall gaps

On the Intracoastal and canal lots, bees move into dock storage boxes, boat lift housings and voids where a seawall cap has cracked. These need a calm approach because people walk right past them to reach the boat; see docks and seawalls.

Palms and landscaped beds

Mature palms with hollow trunks or heavy boot growth, plus irrigation valve boxes buried in the beds, are natural cavities. The person most likely to stumble on one is whoever trims the hedge, so warn your landscaper. A palm colony is usually removed without cutting the palm down.

Bees near the pool deck right now?

Get the kids and guests indoors, then call. A live voice answers at any hour, and anyone already stung is handled before the hive.

Notes on the parts of town

  • Oceanfront lots

    Houses set directly on the Atlantic take the full force of wind and salt spray. Expect worn sealant around windows, roof flashing and stucco joints, and keep an eye on the ocean side of the house, which is the hardest to see from the drive.

  • Ocean Boulevard

    Town Hall sits at 100 Ocean Boulevard, and the early land sales priced Ocean Boulevard plots apart from the oceanfront ones. Before arriving we settle where the van parks, so it does not block a neighbor's gate.

  • The west-side islands

    The town's history describes three islands on the Intracoastal side of town, each with its own bridge. Water on several sides means seawalls, docks and lifts, and a colony on one lot can be a short flight from the next house.

  • The Italian-named avenues

    Terracina, Verona, Ravenna, Palermo, Navona and Massini Avenues carry the Italian names the town gave its streets in its early years. Older homes along them are worth a look at the roofline and any decorative trim.

  • The Beach Pavilion and parks

    The Mediterranean Revival pavilion, the chickee huts and the town's nine parks belong to the town, and its Public Works crews look after the trees there. Report bees in those spots to Town Hall.

Why big, quiet houses suit honey bees

Scouts rate a site on darkness, dryness, volume and a narrow doorway they can defend, and they favor spots that stay undisturbed. Large houses on streets that see little outside traffic tick every box. There are more square feet of soffit, more roof valleys, more hollow decorative trim and more mechanical spaces, and fewer people walking past each one.

That is why a hive here can grow large before anyone reports it. If the home sits empty for part of the year, read our note on bees at a seasonal home, and ask a house manager to watch for a few scout bees circling one spot in spring. Catching a swarm before it builds comb keeps the bill near the low end. The same logic applies to other quiet enclaves such as Key Biscayne and Fisher Island. Once comb is in a wall or a roof, it has to come out completely, or the leftover wax and honey draw new bees back to the same spot. The scent of old comb is the main reason, as this field guide note lays out.

Finding the hive without opening the wrong wall

On a house finished in custom stucco, stone or painted millwork, you do not want anyone guessing. A brood nest runs warmer than the framing around it, and a thermal camera shows that heat as a bright shape behind finished stucco or a ceiling. That lets the cut go exactly where the comb sits, and keep it small. Our page on what thermal imaging shows covers how that works.

The removal itself is done by hand. Comb is cut out, the bees are drawn by a low-suction vacuum into a ventilated box, and a beekeeper takes them in to start over somewhere useful. Closing up is not handed to strangers: the stucco patch, tile reset and touch-up paint are done by licensed people who work for us, matched to what was there, which is the whole point of our repairs after removal. If an owner abroad or a family office wants proof, we can send before-and-after pictures plus a line-by-line invoice.

Storm season, salt air and repeat visits

Hurricanes and tropical storms can peel back roof tile, crack stucco and expose a hive that was hidden for years, here and down the barrier coast through Bal Harbour and Miami Beach. If storm damage turns up comb, keep people back and read bee colonies exposed after a storm before anyone starts pulling debris.

Salt air also wears down sealant faster near the water, so the gaps bees use can reopen on their own. Our workmanship carries a warranty, so should a colony ever reoccupy a repair we made, we return and deal with it. For a full breakdown of what drives the price, see what removal costs.

How a visit runs in Golden Beach

  1. Clear us at the gate

    Every vehicle comes in past the police guardhouse at the town entrance. Let the guardhouse or your house manager know to expect us, with the date and our company name, so the van gets through without a wait.

  2. Look before cutting

    We walk the property with you or your manager, trace the flight line, and scan the wall or roof with a thermal camera to estimate the hive's size and position.

  3. Remove and relocate

    The colony and all comb come out by hand. The bees go to a beekeeper. Every scrap of comb leaves in sealed buckets with us, because a sweet residue would call in the next swarm.

  4. Rebuild and sign off

    Stucco, tile or trim goes back up, done by tradespeople we employ. If the repair needs a town permit, it goes through the Building Department, which currently works by appointment and is closed on weekends.

Questions people ask us

Should we tell the guardhouse you are coming?

It helps. All traffic passes the police guardhouse at the town entrance, so let the guards know we are on the way, or ask your house manager to. A name and a time save a wait at the gate, and it matters most for a night or weekend emergency call.

We are away for months. Can you handle it without us there?

Usually, as long as someone can let us onto the property and clear us at the guardhouse. A house manager, contractor or neighbor can meet us. We send photos of the hive and the repair, and an itemized invoice on request, so you can see the work from wherever you are.

There are bees in a palm at the Beach Pavilion. Who handles that?

The beach, the pavilion and the town parks are town property, and Public Works maintains the trees there. Tell Town Hall or the beach attendants first. If a colony is on your own lot next to the beach, that one is yours, and we can take it out alive.

Will removing a hive from our roof damage the tile?

Some tiles have to be lifted to reach the comb. Our own licensed roofers reset or replace them and seal the gap so bees cannot reuse it. If the same spot ever draws bees again after our repair, the warranty on our workmanship means a return trip.

Is it more expensive to call on a weekend?

Yes. A Sunday-morning or midnight emergency is priced above the same job booked for a Tuesday. When nobody is in danger, waiting for a weekday keeps the price down. The quote is free either way, and you can read about what moves the price before you call.
